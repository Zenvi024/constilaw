The President: office, succession, executive power

Syllabus V.B.1–2(c): who may be President and for how long, the salary and its limits, succession and illness, the prohibitions on the President’s official family, executive power and its unstated residue, control of the executive departments, and supervision of local governments. Finals coverage. On a news card, ask three questions in order: which clause gives the President this power (a specific grant, a statute, or the residual power); over whom (an executive department, which the President controls, or a local government, which the President only supervises); and what limit applies (Sec. 13, the Bill of Rights, local autonomy).

Qualifications, election, term and oath

Art. VII, Sec. 2No person may be elected President unless he is a natural-born citizen of the Philippines, a registered voter, able to read and write, at least forty years of age on the day of the election, and a resident of the Philippines for at least ten years immediately preceding such election.
Art. VII, Sec. 4 (pars. 1–3 and last)The President and the Vice-President shall be elected by direct vote of the people for a term of six years which shall begin at noon on the thirtieth day of June next following the day of the election and shall end at noon of the same date six years thereafter. The President shall not be eligible for any reelection. No person who has succeeded as President and has served as such for more than four years shall be qualified for election to the same office at any time.
No Vice-President shall serve for more than two successive terms. Voluntary renunciation of the office for any length of time shall not be considered as an interruption in the continuity of the service for the full term for which he was elected.
Unless otherwise provided by law, the regular election for President and Vice-President shall be held on the second Monday of May. …
The Supreme Court, sitting en banc, shall be the sole judge of all contests relating to the election, returns, and qualifications of the President or Vice-President, and may promulgate its rules for the purpose.
  • Buenafe v. COMELEC (2022): the qualifications for President are not limited to the five in Sec. 2; statutory grounds (the Omnibus Election Code) apply too. “Eligible” in a certificate of candidacy means having all the qualifications and none of the ineligibilities, so a false claim of eligibility is a material misrepresentation (Sec. 78). But Marcos Jr. was not disqualified: failing to file income tax returns may come from mere neglect, so it is not moral turpitude (tax evasion is); the CA could impose fines alone; and perpetual disqualification is not deemed imposed unless the fallo says so. Election does not waive or moot a lack of qualification. The PET takes over only when a petition is filed with it against a candidate who has been proclaimed, has taken the oath and has assumed office; before that, Rule 64/65 petitions from the COMELEC stay with the Court (which is the same Court anyway).
  • Pormento v. Estrada (2010): the question whether a former President (Estrada, elected 1998, ousted 2001) may run again under the ban on “any reelection” was never answered. Estrada lost in 2010, so the petition was moot, and “capable of repetition yet evading review” failed because Pormento himself was unlikely to face the same action again.
  • Pamatong v. Duterte (2019): a private person’s quo warranto against the President needs a claim to the office (Rule 66, Sec. 5), so only a candidate who ran and lost may bring it, and only within one year (Sec. 11). A nuisance candidate who filed almost two years after the inauguration failed on both counts. The State’s quo warranto is imprescriptible (Republic v. Sereno), a private person’s is not.
  • Natural-born citizenship, the first qualification, is the subject of the two Poe cases on the citizenship map: Tecson v. COMELEC (2004) and Poe-Llamanzares v. COMELEC (2016).
Which remedy, before which body, and when (Buenafe)
  1. Cancel the certificate of candidacy (OEC Sec. 78): a deliberate false material representation (eligibility, age, citizenship, residence); filed within five days after the last day for filing COCs, but not later than 25 days from the filing of the COC. Effect: the person was never a candidate and cannot be substituted.
  2. Disqualification (OEC Secs. 12 and 68): a ground declared by final judgment or found by the COMELEC; filed any day after the last day for filing COCs but not later than proclamation. Effect: the person stops being a candidate but may be substituted if the COC was valid.
  3. After proclamation, oath and assumption: the PET. An election protest (within 30 days of proclamation, by the second or third placer) or quo warranto (within 10 days, by any voter, on ineligibility or disloyalty).
Trap: Sec. 4 has two different bars. “Not eligible for any reelection” binds a person elected President. The four-year rule binds a successor (a Vice-President who became President): more than four years served, never again; four years or less, he may run once. The Vice-President’s own limit is two successive terms. Pormento left open whether “any reelection” also bars a non-immediate run.
Gatmaytan (“Hard Cases Make Bad Movies: The Candidates Poe and Philippine Citizenship Law as Screenplay”, 94 Phil. L.J. 493 (2021)): the two presidential citizenship cases read like a screenplay. Fernando Poe Jr. was the child of aliens and his daughter Grace a foundling; both won in the Supreme Court and were held natural-born, and neither became President (he died while protesting the 2004 result; she placed third in 2016). He rewrites both decisions as scripts to show how the Court decided qualification by presumptions: in Tecson, those who say a candidate is not qualified had to prove it. Assigned under V.B.1(a): expect a question on who bears the burden of proving a presidential candidate’s qualification.

Privileges and salary

Art. VII, Sec. 6The President shall have an official residence. The salaries of the President and Vice-President shall be determined by law and shall not be decreased during their tenure. No increase in said compensation shall take effect until after the expiration of the term of the incumbent during which such increase was approved. They shall not receive during their tenure any other emolument from the Government or any other source.

Sec. 6 makes the salary fixed by law the President’s whole lawful income from office. That is why it matters in the ill-gotten wealth cases: when an officer’s wealth is manifestly out of proportion to salary and other lawful income, the law presumes it was unlawfully acquired.

  • Republic v. Sandiganbayan (2003, the Swiss deposits case): the US$356 million that Ferdinand and Imelda Marcos hid in Swiss foundations was forfeited. Their total salaries from 1966 to 1986 came to ₱2,319,583.33 (US$304,372.43). That was their only known lawful income: they filed no statements of assets and liabilities, and the 1935 and 1973 Constitutions barred the President from receiving any other emolument and from managing a business. The presumption under RA 1379, Sec. 2 arose, and the Marcoses, not the Republic, had to prove other lawful income. Laches and estoppel do not run against the State.
  • Marcos, Jr. v. Republic (2014, Arelma): the Swiss deposits judgment did not end the forfeiture case; the Arelma account at Merrill Lynch in New York could be forfeited by a later separate judgment. Filing the case gives the Sandiganbayan potential jurisdiction over the res abroad; enforcement is a separate, ministerial matter.
  • Immunity from suit has its own section on the Commander-in-Chief map (immunity from suit), but its time limit is settled here: in Estrada v. Arroyo (2001), the Court held that the President is immune only during tenure. The 1987 Constitution did not reenact the 1973 Constitution’s post-tenure immunity, so a former President may be prosecuted for plunder, bribery and graft committed in office, without first being convicted by the Senate.
Trap: a raise for the President is valid but takes effect only after the term of the President who approved it; a cut never applies to the incumbent. Compare Members of Congress (Art. VI, Sec. 10: after the terms of all the Members who approved it) and judges (Art. VIII, Sec. 10: no decrease during continuance in office).
2026 news: (1) VP Sara Duterte asked the Supreme Court (September 2026) to stop three grave-threats cases, claiming “limited procedural immunity” from criminal prosecution as an incumbent impeachable officer. Estrada speaks only of the President’s immunity during tenure, personal to the holder of the office; whether an impeachable Vice-President has any such immunity is the open question (pending when last reported). (2) The only article left in her Senate trial is about unexplained wealth: the same idea as the RA 1379 presumption, but proved for removal, not forfeiture.

Succession, disability and serious illness

Art. VII, Sec. 7 (pars. 2–5, in part)If the President-elect fails to qualify, the Vice-President-elect shall act as President until the President-elect shall have qualified. If a President shall not have been chosen, the Vice-President-elect shall act as President until a President shall have been chosen and qualified. If at the beginning of the term of the President, the President-elect shall have died or shall have become permanently disabled, the Vice-President-elect shall become President. Where no President and Vice-President shall have been chosen or shall have qualified, or where both shall have died or become permanently disabled, the President of the Senate or, in case of his inability, the Speaker of the House of Representatives, shall act as President until a President or a Vice-President shall have been chosen and qualified.
Art. VII, Sec. 8 (par. 1)In case of death, permanent disability, removal from office, or resignation of the President, the Vice-President shall become the President to serve the unexpired term. In case of death, permanent disability, removal from office, or resignation of both the President and Vice-President, the President of the Senate or, in case of his inability, the Speaker of the House of Representatives, shall then act as President until the President or Vice-President shall have been elected and qualified.
Art. VII, Sec. 12In case of serious illness of the President, the public shall be informed of the state of his health. The members of the Cabinet in charge of national security and foreign relations and the Chief of Staff of the Armed Forces of the Philippines, shall not be denied access to the President during such illness.

Two words decide most succession questions. “Becomes” President: a permanent vacancy (death, permanent disability, removal, resignation), and the successor serves the unexpired term. “Acts as” President: a temporary situation (the President-elect fails to qualify, or the President is unable under Sec. 11), and the acting President steps aside when the cause ends. A vacancy in the Vice-Presidency is filled by the President’s nominee from Congress, confirmed by a majority of all the Members of both Houses, voting separately (Sec. 9). If both offices fall vacant, Congress calls a special election (Sec. 10), and temporary inability goes through the four steps of Sec. 11; both are on the other-powers map (canvassing, the special election and disability).

  • Estrada v. Arroyo (2001): resignation is a question of fact: intent to resign plus acts of relinquishment, in any form, judged by the totality of prior, contemporaneous and posterior acts. With no letter, the Court read Estrada’s conduct from the Angara Diary (a snap election he would not run in, negotiations only on the terms of his exit, “Ayoko na”) and his farewell statement (he left “for the sake of peace,” acknowledged Arroyo’s oath, and did not say he would return). RA 3019, Sec. 12 (no resignation while a case is pending) did not bar it. His Sec. 11 letter claiming temporary inability lost to Congress’s recognition of Arroyo, which the Court treated as a political question committed to Congress. EDSA II was intra-constitutional, so the case was justiciable; EDSA I, a revolution, was not.
  • De Leon v. Duterte (2020): Sec. 12 is triggered by serious illness, and the framers left the means of informing the public to the Office of the President (Commissioner Ople: the condition is “left to the President and his doctor”). No ministerial duty, so no mandamus for the President’s medical records; claims based on news reports are hearsay twice removed, and the President was visibly at work. Leonen and Caguioa, JJ., dissented.
Gatmaytan (lecture on De Leon v. Duterte, 2024): “I think the Supreme Court was wrong.” Sec. 12 was written because of Marcos, who would disappear from public view (in 1984 the Palace gave four different explanations in one news story; years later his surgeons confirmed two operations). The ConCom record does create a right: Ople himself said “it is the public’s right to know.” What is ministerial is providing a procedure to release the information, and mandamus also lies to compel a discretionary duty when there is gross abuse of discretion. The Court dismissed by a short notice without even asking the President to comment, and cited televised addresses (which had prompted the concern) as proof of health. He reads it as one more ruling that renders a constitutional check “inert.” He adds that hiding the President’s health is probably not an impeachable culpable violation, but a serious illness can trigger Sec. 8 (permanent disability) or Sec. 11 (inability).
Trap: the Senate President or the Speaker only acts as President (Secs. 7 and 8); they never “become” President. And Sec. 12 gives three officials a right of access to an ill President: the Cabinet members in charge of national security and foreign relations, and the AFP Chief of Staff.

Removal, prohibitions, and the ex officio exception

Art. VII, Sec. 13The President, Vice-President, the Members of the Cabinet, and their deputies or assistants shall not, unless otherwise provided in this Constitution, hold any other office or employment during their tenure. They shall not, during said tenure, directly or indirectly, practice any other profession, participate in any business, or be financially interested in any contract with, or in any franchise, or special privilege granted by the Government or any subdivision, agency, or instrumentality thereof, including government-owned or controlled corporations or their subsidiaries. They shall strictly avoid conflict of interest in the conduct of their office.
The spouse and relatives by consanguinity or affinity within the fourth civil degree of the President shall not, during his tenure, be appointed as Members of the Constitutional Commissions, or the Office of the Ombudsman, or as Secretaries, Undersecretaries, chairmen or heads of bureaus or offices, including government-owned or controlled corporations and their subsidiaries.
Art. IX-B, Sec. 7No elective official shall be eligible for appointment or designation in any capacity to any public office or position during his tenure. Unless otherwise allowed by law or by the primary functions of his position, no appointive official shall hold any other office or employment in the Government or any subdivision, agency or instrumentality thereof, including government-owned or controlled corporations or their subsidiaries.

Removal. The President and the Vice-President may be removed only by impeachment (Art. XI, Sec. 2), for culpable violation of the Constitution, treason, bribery, graft and corruption, other high crimes, or betrayal of public trust; the Vice-President “may be removed from office in the same manner as the President” (Art. VII, Sec. 3). Removal is one of the four causes of a permanent vacancy under Sec. 8. The process is on the other-powers map (impeachment).

  • Civil Liberties Union v. Executive Secretary (1991): Sec. 13 is stricter than Art. IX-B, Sec. 7(2). It covers the President, the Vice-President, Cabinet members and their deputies or assistants (Assistant Secretary and up); it bars any other office, public or private; and its only exceptions are those the Constitution itself provides: the Vice-President as a Cabinet member (Sec. 3), the Vice-President acting as President (Sec. 7), and the Secretary of Justice on the JBC (Art. VIII, Sec. 8(1)). EO 284, which let Cabinet members hold two more posts with pay, was void. Not covered: posts held ex officio, provided by law and required (not merely allowed) by the primary functions of the office, without additional compensation of any kind. They are extra duties, not another office: the Finance Secretary on the Monetary Board. Those who held the invalid posts were de facto officers and kept what they were paid for actual services.
  • Cruz v. COA (2001): the alternates of Secretaries on the NHA Board cannot be paid per diems or allowances, because their principals cannot; “a contrary rule would give petitioners a better right than their principals.”
  • National Amnesty Commission v. COA (2004): the representatives of the ex officio Secretaries on the Amnesty Commission are covered by both provisions: no law created their positions or authorized pay (Art. IX-B, Sec. 7(2)), designation is not appointment, and they cannot have a better right than their principals (Art. VII, Sec. 13). They may attend only as guests: they cannot vote or count toward a quorum. A mere designee is not a de facto officer.

Trap: the ex officio exception has three parts, and a news story usually fails one. (1) By law: a presidential designation alone is not enough. (2) Required by primary functions, not merely allowed or related: the Press Undersecretary on a gaming board fails. (3) Unpaid: any per diem, honorarium or allowance, “by whatever name,” is unconstitutional, for the Secretary, the alternate and the representative alike.

Executive power and the President’s residual powers

Art. VII, Sec. 1The executive power shall be vested in the President of the Philippines.

Sec. 1 vests “the executive power” without defining it. The Court reads it as more than the list in Secs. 14 to 23 (control, appointments, Commander-in-Chief, clemency, loans, treaties, the budget). Whatever inheres in being head of state and head of government belongs to the President unless the Constitution withholds it. Three uses of that residue are on the syllabus: barring a threat to the peace, negotiating peace, and creating a fact-finding body.

  • Marcos v. Manglapus (1989): executive power “is more than the sum of specific powers so enumerated.” President Aquino’s residual power to protect the general welfare, as protector of the peace, let her bar the ailing Marcos and his family from returning. The right involved was not travel or abode but the right to return to one’s country (UDHR Art. 13(2); ICCPR Art. 12(4)), part of the law of the land under Art. II, Sec. 2. Review was limited to whether factual bases existed; they did. The vote was close (seven concurring, six dissenting as printed; later cases call it 8–7), and the dissents argued that liberty of abode yields only to a court and travel only to a law.
  • Province of North Cotabato v. GRP Peace Panel (2008): the power to negotiate peace is implied from the President’s powers as Chief Executive and Commander-in-Chief (citing Manglapus and Sanlakas). The President may explore solutions that need constitutional change and propose them, but has no constituent power: the MOA-AD’s “virtual guarantee” that the Constitution and laws would be changed to fit it was grave abuse.
  • Biraogo v. Philippine Truth Commission of 2010 (2010): the President may create an ad hoc fact-finding body, but the source is the duty to ensure that the laws are faithfully executed (Sec. 17), not the power to reorganize (which alters existing offices), not the power of control (which reviews subordinates’ acts), and not PD 1416 (functus officio since Congress convened). Funding from the OP’s existing appropriations is an allotment, not an appropriation; fact-finding is not adjudication, so the Ombudsman and DOJ are not supplanted. But EO 1 was struck down on equal protection: it deliberately singled out the “previous administration,” one member of the class of past administrations (10–5).
  • DENR v. DENR Region 12 Employees (2003) and Banda v. Ermita (2010) are syllabus cases here too; they are discussed with control and reorganization below.
Gatmaytan (“Judicial Historical Revisionism in the Philippines: Judicial Review and the Rehabilitation of Ferdinand Marcos”, assigned under V.B.2(a)): Manglapus and Ocampo v. Enriquez (2016, the hero’s burial) are “two important cases that touch on the powers of the Philippine President.” In both, the Court upheld the President on executive power (Aquino barring the return; Duterte allowing the burial) and found no grave abuse. His argument is about Ocampo: by saying history is for historians and deciding as if the dictator’s record were not before it, the Court did the historical revisionism itself, made Marcos the hero he wanted to be, and helped refuel the family’s political careers. Courts dealing with mass atrocities have a history-writing function, and the Court abandoned it. On an exam, use him to show that residual executive power can cut both ways: to bar Marcos in 1989 and to honor him in 2016.
Trap: residual power is not a power to legislate. It cannot create offices or appropriate money without a law (Biraogo used existing funds and only a fact-finding body), cannot amend the Constitution (North Cotabato), and must respect the Bill of Rights (Biraogo on equal protection). In Zabal v. Duterte (2019), the Court upheld the Boracay closure on statutes the President implements through control and expressly found no need to rely on residual powers.

Control of the executive departments, and reorganization

Art. VII, Sec. 17The President shall have control of all the executive departments, bureaus, and offices. He shall ensure that the laws be faithfully executed.

Control is the power to alter, modify, nullify or set aside what a subordinate has done in the performance of his duties, and to substitute one’s judgment for the subordinate’s (Mondano v. Silvosa). It reaches every executive employee, from Cabinet Secretary to the lowliest clerk, and the President may exercise it motu proprio, without an appeal. Its corollary is the doctrine of qualified political agency: the Secretaries are the President’s alter egos, and their acts in the regular course of business are presumptively the President’s unless he disapproves them, or the Constitution or law requires him to act in person.

  • Blaquera v. Alcala (1998): capping productivity bonuses at ₱1,000, barring agency heads from granting them without the President’s prior approval, and ordering the excess refunded was valid control, not an intrusion on the Civil Service Commission (fixing amounts is financial management, an executive function). Bodies with fiscal autonomy are not covered. The employees who received the bonuses in good faith need not refund them.
  • NEA v. COA (2002): the control power is self-executing and cannot be limited or withdrawn by Congress; there is only one Chief Executive, and subordinate offices must implement his directives in good faith. NEA, a GOCC, could not pay a salary increase ahead of the two tranches set by EO 389 just because it had the money; an appropriation is not authority to disburse.
  • Hutchison Ports Philippines v. SBMA (2000): the SBMA, a chartered instrumentality, is under the control and supervision of the Office of the President, and contracts of ₱2 million and up need the President’s approval (LOI 620). The President could, for justifiable reasons, set aside the SBMA Board’s award to Hutchison and order a rebidding; an award not yet approved gave the bidder no clear right.
  • Zabal v. Duterte (2019): in approving the NDRRMC’s recommendation to close Boracay, the President exercised control over the executive branch and could directly exercise his subordinates’ functions under the environmental and health laws they implement. The dissent read the same proclamation as an exercise of control over the local governments ordered to implement it, which Art. X, Sec. 4 does not allow.
  • DENR v. DENR Region 12 Employees (2003): under qualified political agency, the DENR Secretary validly ordered the Region XII offices moved from Cotabato City to Koronadal: the President’s continuing power to reorganize may be delegated to the Secretary, and the act stands as the President’s unless he disowns it. Untimeliness (Ramadan, no buildings yet) goes to wisdom, not legality.

Reorganization. The President’s power to restructure the executive branch comes from two places at once: the control power and a statutory delegation (the Administrative Code, Book III, Sec. 31, which lets the President restructure the Office of the President Proper and transfer functions and agencies; the residual powers in Sec. 20; and provisions in the GAAs). It covers only the Executive: not the Judiciary, not Congress, not constitutional bodies.

  • Banda v. Ermita (2010): EO 378, which ended the National Printing Office’s monopoly over government printing (except election materials) and limited its budget to its income, was valid. The express power to abolish, merge and transfer carries the lesser power to modify an agency’s functions. Reorganization must be in good faith, for economy or efficiency; one done to oust employees is void (Dario v. Mison), but whoever alleges bad faith must prove it. Carpio, J., concurring in the result, read Sec. 31 as a closed list and PD 1416 as an undue delegation.
  • Larin v. Executive Secretary (1997) grounded the power to reorganize in the GAA and PD 1416 (which Biraogo later called functus officio), but voided a BIR reorganization for badges of bad faith: offices abolished and recreated to do the same work, and more positions. It is on the appointment map with the power to remove.
  • Biraogo (2010) draws the outer line: reorganizing means altering an existing structure, so creating a new public office is not reorganization; and control is “entirely different from the power to create public offices.”
Gatmaytan (Government Structure, ch. 6, 2015): through the President’s control over the Cabinet, the Secretaries in turn control the bureaus under them, which is how qualified political agency works in practice. But he also quotes a later ruling (Moran v. Office of the President, 2014) that executive control “is not absolute”: the structure of the executive branch and its degrees of control and supervision may be limited by the Constitution, by law, or by judicial decisions (there, the Court’s own rules on appeals). Set that beside NEA v. COA’s line that Congress cannot limit or withdraw control: Congress may define the structure, but not take away the President’s control over what remains executive. The book predates the assigned P&S (2024) and may be outdated.
Trap: control lets the President substitute his judgment; it does not let him create an office (Biraogo), reorganize the Judiciary or a constitutional commission, or bind bodies with fiscal autonomy (Blaquera). And a subordinate cannot shelter behind a statute or its own funds to ignore a valid presidential directive (NEA).

General supervision over local governments and autonomous regions

Art. X, Sec. 4The President of the Philippines shall exercise general supervision over local governments. Provinces with respect to component cities and municipalities, and cities and municipalities with respect to component barangays, shall ensure that the acts of their component units are within the scope of their prescribed powers and functions.
Art. X, Secs. 6 and 16Local government units shall have a just share, as determined by law, in the national taxes which shall be automatically released to them. … The President shall exercise general supervision over autonomous regions to ensure that laws are faithfully executed.

Supervision is oversight: seeing that subordinate officers perform their duties and follow the rules. The supervisor may point out that rules were not followed and order the work redone to conform to them, but may not lay down, modify or replace the rules, or substitute his judgment (Drilon v. Lim). Local officials are elected and answerable to their own voters; Cabinet members are the President’s alter egos. Supervision is not incompatible with discipline: the President may order an investigation of local officials and impose sanctions as the law provides. Local autonomy in the Philippines is decentralization of administration, not of power: policy for the whole country stays with the President and Congress.

  • Pimentel v. Aguirre (2000): AO 372 (1997, during the peso crisis) told all agencies, LGUs included, to cut spending by 25% and withheld 10% of the LGUs’ internal revenue allotment. The spending cut survives only as a non-binding advisory: no sanction may be imposed on LGUs that ignore it. The withholding is void: the just share is released automatically (Art. X, Sec. 6) and “shall not be subject to any lien or holdback … for whatever purpose” (LGC Sec. 286); a temporary withholding is still a holdback. The President may adjust the IRA only under LGC Sec. 284 (an unmanaged public sector deficit; consultation with the presiding officers of Congress and the presidents of the leagues; the recommendation of the Finance, Interior and Budget Secretaries; never below 30%), and none of those steps was shown. 12–3; Kapunan, J., dissenting, would have let the President hold the money back while the deficit was assessed.
  • Zabal v. Duterte (2019): the majority found that the Boracay closure did not intrude on local autonomy; the dissent said ordering the LGUs to implement it was control, citing Pimentel v. Aguirre.
2026 news: the Bangsamoro Autonomous Region held its first parliamentary elections on September 14, 2026, after RA 12317 reset them. Over the BARMM, too, the President has only general supervision “to ensure that laws are faithfully executed” (Art. X, Sec. 16); a national order that tells the regional government how to run its own affairs, or holds back its share, is the Pimentel v. Aguirre problem again.
Trap: the word “direct” or “shall” in a presidential order to LGUs does not settle it. The Court in Pimentel v. Aguirre saved Sec. 1 by reading it as advice; the test is whether a sanction or a holdback backs it up. And “automatically released” does not stop Congress from fixing the share by law (“as determined by law”); it stops the Executive from withholding what the law gives.
