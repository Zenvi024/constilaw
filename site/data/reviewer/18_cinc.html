Commander-in-Chief, other powers, the Vice-President

Syllabus V.B.2(f)–(k) and V.B.3: the Commander-in-Chief clause, from IBP v. Zamora (2000) to the 2019 Lagman ruling on the third extension of martial law in Mindanao; emergency powers; foreign loans; foreign affairs; the President’s part in legislation; immunity from suit; and the Vice-President. Finals coverage. On a Commander-in-Chief news card, ask: which power (calling out, suspending the writ, martial law); on what ground (lawless violence; or actual invasion or rebellion, with public safety); who checks it, how (Congress revokes or extends; the Court reviews the factual basis within 30 days); for how long (60 days, then extensions); and what it cannot do (suspend the Constitution, displace the civil courts, try civilians in military courts).

The Commander-in-Chief and the calling-out power

Art. VII, Sec. 18 (first sentence)The President shall be the Commander-in-Chief of all armed forces of the Philippines and whenever it becomes necessary, he may call out such armed forces to prevent or suppress lawless violence, invasion or rebellion.
Art. II, Sec. 3Civilian authority is, at all times, supreme over the military. The Armed Forces of the Philippines is the protector of the people and the State. Its goal is to secure the sovereignty of the State and the integrity of the national territory.

Sec. 18 gives the President three graduated powers: calling out the armed forces, suspending the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and declaring martial law. The graduation is in scope and effect, not a sequence: the President need not try the lesser power first, and the Court does not choose for the President (Lagman v. Medialdea, 2017). Calling out is the least intrusive and the least checked: its only condition is that it “becomes necessary” to prevent or suppress lawless violence, invasion or rebellion; it needs no report to Congress, no time limit and no act of Congress. The President may not invoke a greater power while using a lesser one (David).

  • IBP v. Zamora (2000): President Estrada deployed Marines to patrol Metro Manila with the police. Calling out is reviewable, but only for grave abuse, and the petitioner must show that the President’s judgment was totally without factual basis. The Court gave its reasons for deference: there are no textual standards, the evidence may not be “technical proof,” much of it is classified, and the President must act fast with a vast intelligence network. Civilian supremacy was not breached because the police stayed in command and the Marines exercised no compulsory power over civilians. The IBP lacked standing but was heard (transcendental importance).
  • Sanlakas v. Executive Secretary (2004): after the Oakwood mutiny, President Arroyo declared a “state of rebellion” and called out the armed forces. The declaration is allowed (the executive power and the duty to execute the laws support it) but is a legal superfluity: it adds no power and cannot justify warrantless arrests beyond the Rules of Court. Moot, but decided as capable of repetition yet evading review. Earlier, in Lacson v. Perez (2001), the Court had said the same of the “state of rebellion” declared after the May 1, 2001 siege of Malacañang: arrests must follow the Rules of Court.
  • Ampatuan v. Puno (2011): after the Maguindanao massacre, a state of emergency in Maguindanao, Sultan Kudarat and Cotabato City, with the AFP and PNP called out, was not an exercise of emergency powers: there was no national emergency and no law, and calling out is the President’s own power. The Court found factual basis in the clans’ armed followers and the threat of retaliation, and no takeover of the ARMM, whose own officials succeeded one another.
  • David v. Macapagal-Arroyo (2006): PP 1017 was valid as a calling-out, but not as a basis for decrees or takeovers (see emergency powers below). The test of review there was arbitrariness, not correctness.
Gatmaytan (Martial Law Cases lecture, Part 2, 2024): in the calling-out cases the Court “would rather defer to the President.” IBP v. Zamora’s list of reasons (no standards, no technical proof, classified information) reads to him as a Court saying “we can’t really do this,” and Ampatuan repeats it: review exists, but “the court isn’t nearly as strong as the Constitution makes it,” because it does not want to contradict the President. Use this for the other side of a deployment question.
Trap: a “state of emergency,” “state of rebellion” or “state of calamity” declared by the President adds no power by itself. It is a calling-out at most (Sanlakas; Ampatuan). Emergency powers and takeovers of businesses need a law (David), and the writ and martial law need actual invasion or rebellion.

Martial law and the writ: grounds, limits and the civil courts

Art. VII, Sec. 18 (par. 1, second sentence)In case of invasion or rebellion, when the public safety requires it, he may, for a period not exceeding sixty days, suspend the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus or place the Philippines or any part thereof under martial law.
Art. VII, Sec. 18 (pars. 4–6)A state of martial law does not suspend the operation of the Constitution, nor supplant the functioning of the civil courts or legislative assemblies, nor authorize the conferment of jurisdiction on military courts and agencies over civilians where civil courts are able to function, nor automatically suspend the privilege of the writ.
The suspension of the privilege of the writ shall apply only to persons judicially charged for rebellion or offenses inherent in or directly connected with invasion.
During the suspension of the privilege of the writ, any person thus arrested or detained shall be judicially charged within three days, otherwise he shall be released.

The 1935 and 1973 texts allowed martial law for invasion, insurrection, rebellion “or imminent danger thereof,” in one short paragraph. The 1987 framers, after fourteen years of Marcos’s martial law, kept the power but fenced it in: two grounds only (actual invasion or rebellion; “imminent danger” and insurrection were deleted), public safety must require it, 60 days at most, a report within 48 hours, Congress may revoke (voting jointly, a majority of all Members) or extend on the President’s initiative, the Court reviews on any citizen’s petition within 30 days, and the four negations of par. 4. Martial law under the 1987 Constitution is not a military government: it lets the military help civilian authorities restore order. “Rebellion” is the crime defined in Art. 134 of the RPC (Lagman, 2017).

  • Olaguer v. Military Commission No. 34 (1987): civilians charged with plotting to assassinate President and Mrs. Marcos were sentenced to death by a military commission after a 25-minute recess. The Court held that military commissions cannot try civilians, even under martial law, while the civil courts are open and functioning; their judgments are void. Due process means trial by judicial process, and commissions are not courts. It abandoned Aquino, Jr. v. Military Commission No. 2 (1975), and pointed to Sec. 18, par. 4 as the new text. Even with jurisdiction, a tribunal that denies constitutional rights loses it (Gumabon).
  • In re De Villa (2004): the writ of habeas corpus tests the legality of a restraint, not the correctness of a judgment. After a final conviction it lies only for (a) a denied constitutional right resulting in the restraint, (b) a court without jurisdiction to impose the sentence, or (c) an excessive penalty (as to the excess). DNA tests disproving the convict’s paternity of the victim’s child fit none of these. Carpio, J., concurring, would let the Court order a new trial when DNA evidence unavailable at trial shows the convict could not have committed the crime.
  • Fortun v. Macapagal-Arroyo (2012): the first martial law case under the 1987 Constitution (Maguindanao, December 2009) was dismissed as moot, because the proclamation was lifted after eight days, before Congress voted. Carpio, J., dissenting, set out what Lagman later adopted: rebellion means Art. 134 of the RPC; probable cause is enough; martial law does not authorize warrantless arrests outside Rule 113, and suspending the writ does not validate an illegal arrest, it only suspends the remedy.

How the Court got here. Montenegro v. Castañeda (1952) held the suspension of the writ final and unreviewable; Lansang v. Garcia (1971) made it reviewable for arbitrariness; Garcia-Padilla v. Enrile (1983) returned to Montenegro (the President answers “only to his conscience, the people and to God”). The 1987 Constitution adopted Lansang and went further: any citizen may sue, and the Court must decide within 30 days.

Gatmaytan (Martial Law Cases, Part 1): Marcos’s martial law lasted 14 years, “a very long time for a legal remedy that’s supposed to last a very short time,” with 3,257 murders, about 35,000 tortured and 70,000 arrested. Sec. 18 “doesn’t even fit into a single slide” because the framers, many of them lawyers, wrote in every restriction they could so “we do not suffer the same fate.” The line on military courts “is meant to address” the martial-law rulings that let military tribunals try the opposition; the three-day rule stops detention without charges.
Trap: martial law does not automatically suspend the writ, and suspending the writ does not legalize arrests: arrests still need a warrant or a Rule 113 ground. The suspension covers only persons judicially charged for rebellion or offenses inherent in or directly connected with invasion, and they must be charged within three days or released.

Reviewing martial law: the Court, Congress and extensions

Art. VII, Sec. 18 (par. 3)The Supreme Court may review, in an appropriate proceeding filed by any citizen, the sufficiency of the factual basis of the proclamation of martial law or the suspension of the privilege of the writ or the extension thereof, and must promulgate its decision thereon within thirty days from its filing.

The three checks run separately: Congress (revoke at any time, on any ground; extend on the President’s initiative), the Court (the sufficiency of the factual basis, on a citizen’s petition), and the 60-day clock. Congress need not meet in joint session unless it votes to revoke or extend (Padilla v. Congress, 2017, on the other-powers map).

  • Lagman v. Medialdea (2017): Proclamation No. 216 (May 23, 2017; the Marawi siege) put all of Mindanao under martial law. The Court upheld it, 11–4, and set the rules of review. The proceeding is sui generis, not certiorari, and does not wait for Congress (Fortun set aside). The question is sufficiency, not accuracy: (1) actual rebellion or invasion, and (2) public safety, which must concur, with (3) probable cause for the President to believe them. The Court looks only at the facts known to the President at the time (the Proclamation and the Report), in their totality; some false or inaccurate facts do not void it if the rest suffice. The Court does not calibrate the choice of power or the area: rebellion has no predetermined bounds, so all of Mindanao could be covered. Carpio, J. would have confined it to Marawi; Sereno, C.J. and Caguioa, J. to Lanao del Sur, Maguindanao and Sulu; Leonen, J. would have voided it. On reconsideration (December 5, 2017), the Court called the 60-day limit a “time fuse” and the extension a separate act.
  • Lagman v. Pimentel III (2018): Congress may extend more than once, for any period it fixes; “in the same manner” means the joint vote by a majority of all Members, not 60 days. The only limits are the President’s initiative, persistence of the rebellion with public safety, and the Court’s review. A rebellion persists after the fighting stops (recruitment, financing, regrouping; it is a continuing crime), and other groups, even the NPA, may be counted. The President cannot be impleaded; how Congress deliberated (three-minute interpellations) is its own business. 10–5.
  • Lagman v. Medialdea (2019): the third extension was upheld, 9–4: the test is reasonableness, not accuracy; the Court need not make its own findings; Proclamation No. 216 did not lapse when Marawi was freed; the manner of Congress’s approval is a political question; and rights abuses must be pursued in separate cases (RA 7438, RA 9745, amparo, habeas data), not by voiding the extension.
Gatmaytan (Martial Law Cases, Parts 3 and 4): Lagman rightly corrected Fortun, but its rule that falsities in the report do not matter “really shook me to the core”: “you can have a report that’s 90% wrong” and still find a sufficient basis. Padilla lets the President’s allies pass resolutions of support and skip the joint session, and Lagman v. Pimentel III lets Congress extend for any length (“14 years, 28 years, it doesn’t matter”). His conclusion: “constitutional constraints have been rendered inert by the Supreme Court,” and none of these decisions was unanimous, so “it matters who we appoint.” In “The Philippines’ Authoritarian Constitution,” 95 Phil. L.J. 529 (2022), he says the Court erased “virtually every constitutional safeguard” on martial law.
Trap: the Court reviews only the sufficiency of the factual basis, not grave abuse and not wisdom; Congress may revoke for any reason, wisdom included. And the Court’s 30 days run from the filing of the petition, not from the proclamation.

Emergency powers

Art. VI, Sec. 23(2)In times of war or other national emergency, the Congress may, by law, authorize the President, for a limited period and subject to such restrictions as it may prescribe, to exercise powers necessary and proper to carry out a declared national policy. Unless sooner withdrawn by resolution of the Congress, such powers shall cease upon the next adjournment thereof.
Art. XII, Sec. 17In times of national emergency, when the public interest so requires, the State may, during the emergency and under reasonable terms prescribed by it, temporarily take over or direct the operation of any privately owned public utility or business affected with public interest.

Emergency powers are Congress’s to lend: they are delegated legislative power, by law, for a limited period, with restrictions, to carry out a declared policy. The President’s own emergency tools are the Commander-in-Chief powers. The three provisions are compared on the other-powers map.

  • David v. Macapagal-Arroyo (2006): PP 1017 declared a state of national emergency, ordered the AFP to enforce “all decrees … promulgated by me” (Marcos’s PP 1081 again), and invoked the takeover clause. The Court: the President alone may declare a national emergency and call out the armed forces; but she cannot issue decrees (legislative power is Congress’s), and the takeover of private businesses under Art. XII, Sec. 17 needs a law, because “the State” there means Congress, read with Art. VI, Sec. 23(2). G.O. No. 5 was valid except its undefined “acts of terrorism”; the warrantless arrests, dispersals and the raid on the Daily Tribune were unconstitutional. An emergency may be economic, natural or security-related, not only a typhoon.
  • Ampatuan v. Puno (2011): a local state of emergency with troops called out is not an exercise of emergency powers.
  • RA 12287 (2025), the Declaration of State of Imminent Disaster Act: the President, on the NDRRMC’s recommendation based on a pre-disaster risk assessment, may declare a State of Imminent Disaster over clusters of barangays, municipalities, cities, provinces or regions (local chief executives may do so in their areas). The criteria: a forecasted hazard (flood, typhoon, drought, heat wave, storm surge) with severe projected impacts, and a lead time of three days (up to five) for anticipatory action. It allows pre-emptive evacuation, prepositioning of goods and teams, social amelioration and the release of funds; it is lifted if the projected impacts are no longer expected, and automatically when the hazard strikes. It is a statute giving the Executive defined tools, not a Sec. 23(2) delegation of legislative power and not a takeover authority.
  • The Bayanihan to Heal as One Act (RA 11469, 2020) is the modern example of Sec. 23(2) and the takeover clause used together: a law, for a limited period, with listed powers, including the power to take over or direct hospitals and transport.
Gatmaytan: David is “a rare win” against the State: the President tried to legislate and to take over businesses, and the Court said both need Congress (Martial Law Cases, Part 2). On the Bayanihan Act (Rappler interview, 2020): the Commander-in-Chief powers did not apply, since there was no invasion or rebellion; the law invoked Sec. 23(2) and the takeover power, for a limited period that Congress may cut short by resolution; and many of its powers the President already had under other laws.
Trap: “emergency” does not make the President a legislator. Declaring an emergency: the President alone. Exercising emergency powers or taking over a business: only under a law. And the delegated powers end at the next adjournment of Congress unless withdrawn sooner by resolution.

Contracting and guaranteeing foreign loans

Art. VII, Sec. 20The President may contract or guarantee foreign loans on behalf of the Republic of the Philippines with the prior concurrence of the Monetary Board, and subject to such limitations as may be provided by law. The Monetary Board shall, within thirty days from the end of every quarter of the calendar year, submit to the Congress a complete report of its decisions on applications for loans to be contracted or guaranteed by the Government or government-owned and controlled corporations which would have the effect of increasing the foreign debt, and containing other matters as may be provided by law.
Art. XII, Sec. 21Foreign loans may only be incurred in accordance with law and the regulation of the monetary authority. Information on foreign loans obtained or guaranteed by the Government shall be made available to the public.

The 1973 Constitution had no Monetary Board check. With the foreign debt at US$26 billion, the 1987 framers required the central bank’s prior concurrence, because it keeps the reserves and knows the country’s paying capacity. The enabling laws include RA 4860 (the Foreign Borrowings Act, 1966), which authorizes the President to borrow from foreign governments, agencies and institutions for development projects, and RA 245.

  • Constantino v. Cuisia (2005): the 1992 debt-relief program (a cash buyback of debt at a discount; old debt converted into bonds) was within Sec. 20, which is broad: a bond is a loan, and a buyback is an implied incident of borrowing. The Finance Secretary may exercise the power as the President’s alter ego, with the President’s approval presumed; only powers that suspend freedoms or override other branches (martial law, the writ, pardon) must be exercised personally. Whether the deals waived the right to repudiate behest loans was not ripe.
  • Colmenares v. Duterte (2022): the Chinese loans for the Chico River irrigation project (US$62 million) and the Kaliwa Dam (US$211 million) were valid. “Prior concurrence” works in three stages: an Approval-in-Principle before negotiations, review of the documents, and a Final Approval after signing but before any drawdown. The confidentiality clause, as worded, could not stand against Art. XII, Sec. 21 (moot; guidance). Limiting the bidding to three Chinese contractors apparently contravened the Filipino First policy, but the loans themselves did not. Leonen, J., dissenting: without a final contract the Board cannot concur, so Final Approval must come before signing.
Trap: the Monetary Board concurs; Congress does not, though the Board reports to it every quarter. And “prior” concurrence does not mean full approval before signing: the Approval-in-Principle comes before, the Final Approval before drawdown (Colmenares).

Foreign affairs

Art. XVIII, Sec. 25After the expiration in 1991 of the Agreement between the Republic of the Philippines and the United States of America concerning Military Bases, foreign military bases, troops, or facilities shall not be allowed in the Philippines except under a treaty duly concurred in by the Senate and, when the Congress so requires, ratified by a majority of the votes cast by the people in a national referendum held for that purpose, and recognized as a treaty by the other contracting State.

The President is the chief architect of foreign policy and the country’s sole representative with foreign nations: the President negotiates and ratifies treaties, and only the Senate’s concurrence is needed (Art. VII, Sec. 21; Pimentel v. Executive Secretary). The President decides whether an agreement is a treaty or an executive agreement, within limits (Saguisag; Bayan Muna), and the power over aliens, including deportation, is part of this field. Treaties and withdrawal are on the other-powers map.

  • Nicolas v. Romulo (2009): US Marine Daniel Smith, convicted of rape, was moved from the Makati City Jail to the US Embassy under the Romulo-Kenney Agreements. The VFA itself satisfies Sec. 25: the Senate concurred and the US recognized it as a treaty; whether the US Senate ratified it is US law, and US troops are also here under the Mutual Defense Treaty, which both Senates ratified. Medellin v. Texas changes nothing. Custody during trial may stay with the US (no breach of equal protection or of the Court’s rule-making power). But after conviction the VFA requires detention by Philippine authorities in agreed facilities, so the embassy arrangement was not in accord with the VFA. 9–4; Puno, C.J. and Carpio, J. dissented: the US treats the VFA as a mere executive agreement, so it is not “recognized as a treaty.”
  • Go Tek v. Deportation Board (1977): the President may deport an undesirable alien, after investigation under Sec. 69 of the Revised Administrative Code, on grounds not listed in the Immigration Law, and without waiting for a criminal conviction. Deportation is an attribute of sovereignty; the Deportation Board only investigates and recommends. Due process means notice, time to prepare, counsel and the right to present and cross-examine witnesses.
2026 news: the Senate concurred in the Philippines–France Status of Visiting Forces Agreement on October 8, 2026. For a news card, run it through Sec. 25 as in Nicolas: a treaty, Senate concurrence, and recognition as a treaty by France; then ask who holds a French soldier accused or convicted here. The ICC’s confirmation of charges against former President Duterte (April 2026) and the warrant against Sen. dela Rosa (May 2026) raise the withdrawal and cooperation questions on the other-powers map.
Trap: Sec. 25 asks whether the other State recognizes the agreement as a treaty, not whether its own Senate ratified it (Nicolas, reaffirming the 2000 VFA ruling). And deportation is not a criminal case: no conviction is needed, and an acquittal does not bar it (Go Tek).

The President’s part in legislation

Art. VII, Secs. 22 and 23The President shall submit to the Congress within thirty days from the opening of every regular session, as the basis of the general appropriations bill, a budget of expenditures and sources of financing, including receipts from existing and proposed revenue measures.
The President shall address the Congress at the opening of its regular session. He may also appear before it at any other time.

The syllabus lists four legislative roles and no cases; each is covered where its doctrine lives. (1) Address Congress: the State of the Nation Address at the opening of each regular session (the fourth Monday of July); the President may appear at any other time. (2) The budget: within 30 days from the opening of the regular session, as the basis of the general appropriations bill; Congress may not increase the appropriations the President recommends for the operation of the Government (Art. VI, Sec. 25(1)); see the appropriation provisions. (3) The veto: of whole bills, and of items in appropriation, revenue or tariff bills; overridden by two-thirds of all the Members of each House; see the veto. (4) Emergency powers delegated under Art. VI, Sec. 23(2): see above.

Trap: the President proposes the budget; only Congress appropriates. And the 30 days for submitting the budget run from the opening of the regular session, not from the start of the fiscal year.

Immunity from suit

The 1987 Constitution says nothing about presidential immunity; the 1973 Constitution, as amended in 1981, had written it in, for acts during tenure and, after tenure, for official acts. The framers left it out because it was “understood in present jurisprudence” (Bernas). Its reasons: the President must work free of hindrance or distraction, the office’s dignity, and the remedy of impeachment. Do not confuse it with the State’s immunity from suit.

  • De Lima v. Duterte (2019): Sen. De Lima’s petition for habeas data against President Duterte, over his public attacks on her, was dismissed because the incumbent President is immune from suit during tenure: any suit, on official or personal acts, even habeas data. The US rule confining immunity to official acts does not apply; there is no balancing test; and the Court applies immunity even if the President does not invoke it. Her remedy is impeachment, or a suit after the tenure. The syllabus also flags Leonen, J.’s dissent on reconsideration (November 3, 2020).
  • Soliven v. Makasiar (1988; the syllabus’s “Beltran v. Macasiar”): immunity belongs to the President and may be invoked only by the President; an accused in a libel case filed by President Aquino could not raise it, and the President may waive it and sue. Also: the judge must personally evaluate the prosecutor’s report before issuing a warrant, not personally examine witnesses.
  • Gloria v. Court of Appeals (2000): a suit against the Education Secretary and a Regional Director to stop a reassignment the President approved is not a suit against the President: the acts are theirs, and presidential decisions are reviewable for grave abuse anyway.
  • Estrada v. Arroyo (2001): immunity lasts only during tenure; a former President may be prosecuted for crimes committed in office.
  • In practice, the President is dropped as a respondent while the officials who carry out the act stay: David (2006), Lagman v. Pimentel III (2018) and Colmenares v. Duterte (2022).
2026 news: VP Sara Duterte asked the Supreme Court (September 2026) to stop three grave-threats cases, claiming “limited procedural immunity” from criminal prosecution as an incumbent impeachable officer; the Quezon City court deferred her arraignment on September 11. The cases above speak only of the President’s immunity, personal to the holder of the office (Soliven) and grounded in the President’s unique duties (De Lima); no ruling extends it to the Vice-President, and the question was pending when last reported.
Trap: immunity is a shield for the President, not for the President’s officials (Gloria) and not a defense for someone the President sued (Soliven). It ends with the tenure (Estrada), and it does not stop impeachment.

The Vice-President

Art. VII, Sec. 3There shall be a Vice-President who shall have the same qualifications and term of office and be elected with and in the same manner as the President. He may be removed from office in the same manner as the President.
The Vice-President may be appointed as a Member of the Cabinet. Such appointment requires no confirmation.
Art. VII, Sec. 4 (pars. 3 and 7)No Vice-President shall serve for more than two successive terms. Voluntary renunciation of the office for any length of time shall not be considered as an interruption in the continuity of the service for the full term for which he was elected.
The Supreme Court, sitting en banc, shall be the sole judge of all contests relating to the election, returns, and qualifications of the President or Vice-President, and may promulgate its rules for the purpose.

The Vice-President shares the President’s qualifications (natural-born, a registered voter, able to read and write, at least 40, a resident for ten years), the six-year term, the election and canvass by Congress, and removal only by impeachment; unlike the President, the Vice-President may be re-elected once. Privileges and salary: fixed by law, not decreased during tenure, no other emolument (Sec. 6). Prohibitions: the Sec. 13 rules bind the Vice-President too (no other office, no practice of profession, no financial interest in government contracts), except a Cabinet post, which needs no confirmation (Sec. 3). Succession: the Vice-President becomes President on a permanent vacancy and acts as President when the President-elect fails to qualify or is unable; a vacancy in the Vice-Presidency is filled by a nominee from Congress confirmed by a majority of all the Members of both Houses voting separately (Sec. 9). See qualifications, privileges, prohibitions and succession on the President map.

  • Marcos, Jr. v. Robredo (2021, PET): Marcos’s protest against VP Robredo’s 2016 win (by 263,473 votes) was dismissed, 15–0 to dismiss. An election protest must specify each irregularity and its precinct. Under PET Rule 65 the protestant picks at most three pilot provinces; if their revision shows no reasonable recovery of votes (here Robredo’s lead grew to 278,566), the protest is dismissed without going further, and that also bars the cause of action to annul elections, which would fail anyway (the Abayon requisites). The PET may annul election results as an incident of its judicial power but cannot call special elections, which belong to the COMELEC. RA 1793’s 20-month period no longer binds the PET. The Tribunal itself is on the judiciary map (Batch 8).
2026 news: VP Sara Duterte’s second impeachment (May 2026) went to trial in the Senate, where only the unexplained-wealth article remained by October; the whole story is on the other-powers map. Her claim of immunity from criminal prosecution as Vice-President is in the immunity section above.
Trap: the Vice-President may serve two successive terms; the President, one, with no re-election ever. And a Cabinet appointment of the Vice-President needs no CA confirmation, but other offices are barred by Sec. 13.
