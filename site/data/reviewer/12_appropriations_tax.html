Appropriation and tax laws

Syllabus V.A.8(a)(2): appropriation laws (Demetria to Madera) and tax laws (Tolentino to John Hay). 16 cases. Tested in 2013 (DAP-type realignment; PDAF veto), 2015 (GAA after Belgica and Araullo; tax bill origin), 2016 (confidential and intelligence funds), 2020 finals (PAO forensic lab rider).

The provisions

Art. VI, Secs. 24, 25(1), 25(2), 25(5), 25(7), 29(1)Section 24. All appropriation, revenue or tariff bills, bills authorizing increase of the public debt, bills of local application, and private bills, shall originate exclusively in the House of Representatives, but the Senate may propose or concur with amendments.
Section 25. (1) The Congress may not increase the appropriations recommended by the President for the operation of the Government as specified in the budget. The form, content, and manner of preparation of the budget shall be prescribed by law. (2) No provision or enactment shall be embraced in the general appropriations bill unless it relates specifically to some particular appropriation therein. Any such provision or enactment shall be limited in its operation to the appropriation to which it relates. … (5) No law shall be passed authorizing any transfer of appropriations; however, the President, the President of the Senate, the Speaker of the House of Representatives, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court, and the heads of Constitutional Commissions may, by law, be authorized to augment any item in the general appropriations law for their respective offices from savings in other items of their respective appropriations. … (7) If, by the end of any fiscal year, the Congress shall have failed to pass the general appropriations bill for the ensuing fiscal year, the general appropriations law for the preceding fiscal year shall be deemed re-enacted and shall remain in force and effect until the general appropriations bill is passed by the Congress.
Section 29. (1) No money shall be paid out of the Treasury except in pursuance of an appropriation made by law.

Other limits: Sec. 25(3) (Congress’s own appropriations follow the same procedure), 25(4) (special appropriations must specify the purpose and be supported by funds actually available), 25(6) (discretionary funds only for public purposes, with vouchers and guidelines), 29(2) (no public money for any church or religious dignitary, with listed exceptions), 29(3) (taxes for a special purpose form a special fund), and the item veto (Sec. 27(2)).

The pork barrel line: how doctrine changed

  1. Philconsa v. Enriquez (1994): Countrywide Development Fund upheld (legislators only recommend). Inappropriate provisions may be vetoed separately; conditions cannot be vetoed without the item.
  2. LAMP (2012): PDAF upheld on the same premise.
  3. Belgica (2013): reversal. The PDAF and similar post-enactment powers of legislators are unconstitutional (separation of powers, non-delegation, checks and balances via the item veto, accountability, local autonomy). Philconsa and LAMP abandoned on this point.
  4. Belgica (2019): lump sums are not automatically pork; valid if they meet singular correspondence or have adequate standards.
Singular correspondence (Belgica 2013; 2019)
  1. A valid item allocates a specified amount for a specified purpose, so the President can veto it as a unit.
  2. A lump sum is valid if all its sub-uses fall under one purpose, or if it has standards fixing its use.
  3. Invalid: a lump sum for several purposes where the amounts and projects are chosen after enactment (especially by legislators).

Transfers, savings and augmentation

Art. VI, Sec. 25(5) requisites (Demetria; Araullo)
  1. A law (usually the GAA) authorizes the officer to augment.
  2. There are actual savings: the purpose was completed or abandoned, or efficiencies achieved, after the item was fully provided for. Unreleased or unobligated funds declared ‘savings’ mid-year are not savings.
  3. The transfer augments an existing item in the GAA (not a new project with no appropriation).
  4. The transfer stays within the officer’s own branch or office (no ‘cross-border’ transfers).
  • Demetria v. Alba (1987): a blanket authority to transfer any fund to any project is void.
  • Araullo (2014): DAP partly void (withdrawn allotments as ‘savings’; cross-border transfers; unprogrammed funds without excess revenue; projects with no appropriation). Operative fact for projects done, not for authors absent findings of good faith.
  • Pichay (2012): the President may reorganize his own office and realign its funds under the GAA.
  • Nazareth v. Villar (2013): a benefits law without an appropriation cannot be paid; savings cannot augment a non-existent item.
  • Macalintal v. COMELEC (2023): a law postponing elections to free election funds for other uses was a disguised transfer of appropriations and void.
  • Dela Cruz v. Ochoa (2018): an item for a major final output covers its integral components.
  • Madera (2020): who refunds disallowed amounts (good-faith officers do not; recipients generally do, subject to exceptions).
  • Guingona v. Carague (1991): automatic appropriations for debt service are valid; education’s ‘highest priority’ does not bar them.
In a news question: (1) Confidential and intelligence funds (2016 midterm; the OVP controversy): they must be appropriated by law for a specific office and purpose; transferring them to another office mid-year is a transfer of appropriations; the Senate may delete or reduce them (Congress may decrease, never increase, the President’s recommendations); they remain subject to COA audit. (2) A GAA provision defunding a unit (2020 finals, PAO forensic lab): a condition that relates to the agency’s appropriation is not a rider; Congress may refuse to fund. A provision amending other laws or the agency’s structure is an inappropriate provision (Philconsa) that the President may veto. (3) A GAA provision letting DBM change agencies’ organizational structures: substantive legislation in a budget law relates to no particular appropriation and is a rider under Sec. 25(2).
Traps: (1) Congress may reduce or delete but may not increase the President’s recommended appropriations for the operation of government (Sec. 25(1)). (2) The re-enacted budget applies automatically if no GAA passes (Sec. 25(7)). (3) The President’s item veto applies only to appropriation, revenue and tariff bills; elsewhere the veto is all-or-nothing.

Taxation

Art. VI, Sec. 28(1) The rule of taxation shall be uniform and equitable. The Congress shall evolve a progressive system of taxation. (2) The Congress may, by law, authorize the President to fix within specified limits, and subject to such limitations and restrictions as it may impose, tariff rates, import and export quotas, tonnage and wharfage dues, and other duties or imposts within the framework of the national development program of the Government. (3) Charitable institutions, churches and parsonages or convents appurtenant thereto, mosques, non-profit cemeteries, and all lands, buildings, and improvements, actually, directly, and exclusively used for religious, charitable, or educational purposes shall be exempt from taxation. (4) No law granting any tax exemption shall be passed without the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of the Congress.
  • Origination (Tolentino v. Sec. of Finance): the bill, not the law, must originate in the House; the Senate may propose amendments, even a substitute. The bicameral committee may add germane provisions. A presidential certification of urgency dispenses with both three separate readings and printing three days before.
  • Uniformity and equity: taxes on persons and things of the same class at the same rate; reasonable classification allowed. The progressive taxation clause is a directive, not an enforceable right (Tolentino).
  • Exemptions: only Congress grants them, by a majority of all Members, expressly (John Hay PAC: no exemption by presidential proclamation). Constitutional exemption for charitable property requires actual, direct and exclusive use; leased commercial portions are taxable (Lung Center).
  • Tariff delegation: valid within limits Congress sets (Garcia v. Executive Secretary).
2015 midterm (income tax reform pushed by the House, opposed by the President): a revenue bill must originate in the House, which it did. The President’s objections are relevant only when the bill reaches him: he may then veto it (or particular items, since it is a revenue bill), and Congress may override by two-thirds of all members of each House.
