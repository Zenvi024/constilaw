The Supreme Court

Syllabus V.C.1–2(o): judicial power, then the Supreme Court itself: how it sits, who may be appointed, what its Members may not do, their salary, tenure and removal, the Judiciary’s fiscal autonomy (with RA 12233), jurisdiction, deliberations, voting, what a decision must contain, motions for reconsideration, the periods for deciding, and the Presidential Electoral Tribunal. Finals coverage. On a judiciary news card, ask: which guarantee of independence is touched (appointment, tenure, salary, budget, jurisdiction, the Court’s own deliberations), who is trying to reach into the Court, and what the text allows. Judicial review itself (standing, actual case, political questions) is on the judicial review map.

Judicial power, composition and modes of sitting

Art. VIII, Sec. 1The judicial power shall be vested in one Supreme Court and in such lower courts as may be established by law. Judicial power includes the duty of the courts of justice to settle actual controversies involving rights which are legally demandable and enforceable, and to determine whether or not there has been a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of the Government.
Art. VIII, Sec. 4(1)The Supreme Court shall be composed of a Chief Justice and fourteen Associate Justices. It may sit en banc or in its discretion, in divisions of three, five, or seven Members. Any vacancy shall be filled within ninety days from the occurrence thereof.

The second sentence of Sec. 1 is the 1987 innovation: the expanded power to check grave abuse by “any branch or instrumentality,” written to keep the political question doctrine from shielding the political branches as it did under martial law. There is one Supreme Court: a Division is not a separate court, and its decision is the decision of the Court. What a Division may not do is set by the text: cases on the constitutionality of a treaty, international or executive agreement, or law go to the en banc (Sec. 4(2)); a doctrine may be modified or reversed only by the en banc (Sec. 4(3)); and only the en banc may order a lower-court judge’s dismissal (Sec. 11).

  • People v. Gacott, Jr. (1995): a Division reprimanded and fined a judge ₱10,000 for gross ignorance of the law. He argued that only the en banc may discipline judges. The Court held, 14–0, that Sec. 11 requires the full Court only to dismiss; the en banc may set the procedure for the rest (Bar Matter No. 209 reserved for it dismissal, suspension over one year, and fines over ₱10,000). A Division’s decision, by a majority of those who took part and voted, and at least three, is the Supreme Court’s.
Gatmaytan (“Judicial Docility,” 2025 lecture): the framers expanded judicial power so that the Court would be a stronger check on the President, but “judicial deference to the president is making a stronger chief executive and frustrating constitutional design.” His examples: the Court accepting presidential certifications of urgent bills (Tolentino), refusing to compel disclosure of the President’s health (De Leon v. Duterte), and upholding martial law on reports that need not be accurate (Lagman). On an exam, he wants you to point to a specific provision the other branch violated: then review becomes the Court’s duty.
Trap: a Division’s ruling is not “appealable” to the en banc; the en banc takes a case from a Division only when the Division cannot muster the votes, when a doctrine would be modified or reversed, or when the Court’s rules refer it there.

Appointments and qualifications

Art. VIII, Sec. 7(1) and (3)No person shall be appointed Member of the Supreme Court or any lower collegiate court unless he is a natural-born citizen of the Philippines. A Member of the Supreme Court must be at least forty years of age, and must have been for fifteen years or more, a judge of a lower court or engaged in the practice of law in the Philippines. … A Member of the Judiciary must be a person of proven competence, integrity, probity, and independence.
Art. VIII, Sec. 9The Members of the Supreme Court and judges of lower courts shall be appointed by the President from a list of at least three nominees prepared by the Judicial and Bar Council for every vacancy. Such appointments need no confirmation. For the lower courts, the President shall issue the appointments within ninety days from the submission of the list.

Four filters: natural-born citizenship, age and experience, the character qualifications of Sec. 7(3), and a JBC list. The appointment needs no Commission on Appointments confirmation. Qualifications are not left to the JBC and the President alone: the Court will rule on them in a proper case, before the appointee takes office (Kilosbayan) or after (Sereno, by quo warranto). The 90 days for filling a vacancy in the Court run from the vacancy (Sec. 4(1)); the election ban on appointments does not reach the Court (De Castro v. JBC, on the appointment map). The JBC itself is in Batch 10.

  • Kilosbayan Foundation v. Ermita (2007): the President appointed Gregory Ong, whose own 1979 bar petition said he became Filipino through his father’s naturalization while he was a minor. The Court enjoined him, 13–0, from accepting the post until he proves in an adversarial court proceeding that he is natural-born: one who acquires citizenship through a parent’s naturalization is naturalized, and BI and DOJ papers cannot amend a final naturalization ruling (a change in the civil register needs a Rule 108 order).
  • Republic v. Sereno (2018): proven integrity is a constitutional qualification, and filing SALNs is evidence of it. Sereno’s failure to file and submit them showed she lacked it; neither the JBC’s nomination nor the appointment cured the defect, and she was ousted by quo warranto (8–6). See Removal below.
Gatmaytan (“Elitism of SC Nominees,” 2024): the JBC was created by the 1987 Constitution against the politicized selection of Justices under martial law, but after its first 20 years the Court is “still an elitist institution”: over 60% of appointees come from the judiciary (mostly the Court of Appeals and the Sandiganbayan), most are men in their mid-60s, women are under 20% of nominees and appointees, and religion or ethnic origin is not considered. Appointing Justices near 70 means fast turnover; one served less than a year.
2026 news: the JBC opened the search for a Chief Justice on August 29, 2026, ahead of Chief Justice Gesmundo’s compulsory retirement at 70 on November 6; it sent five nominees for Chief Justice and eleven for an associate seat on October 29. The President appointed Justice Rodil Zalameda the 28th Chief Justice on November 5, a day before the vacancy, and he took his oath on November 6. For a news card: Sec. 9 (a JBC list of at least three), Sec. 4(1) (90 days), Sec. 11 (retirement at 70), and whether an appointment made before the vacancy occurs is valid (the vacancy question under Velicaria-Garafil, on the appointment map).
Trap: for the Supreme Court the 90 days run from the vacancy (Sec. 4(1)); for lower courts, from the JBC’s submission of the list (Sec. 9). And natural-born citizenship is required of Members of every lower collegiate court too, but the age and 15-year rule in Sec. 7(1) is for the Supreme Court.

No non-judicial work for judges

Art. VIII, Sec. 12The Members of the Supreme Court and of other courts established by law shall not be designated to any agency performing quasi-judicial or administrative functions.

Judges do judicial work. The rule protects both the separation of powers and the Court’s independence: a judge sitting in an agency would make rulings that someone other than the higher courts could review, or rulings he himself would later review on appeal. Sec. 12 put into the text what the cases already held.

  • Meralco v. Pasay Transportation Co. (1932): a statute made the Justices a board of arbitrators to fix what one utility pays another for using a bridge, with a final award. The Justices declined, 11–0. The Court as an entity is not its Members; as arbitrators they would exercise either judicial power outside the Court’s jurisdiction (which they would then review) or administrative power, which they cannot take on.
  • Garcia v. Macaraig, Jr. (1971): a newly appointed judge whose court could not yet function worked at the DOJ while drawing his salary. The complaint was dismissed, 7–0 (he had a “perfect right” to his salary once he assumed office), but the Court said it “feels very strongly” that detailing judges to the Department of Justice must stop: no judge should be where anyone but the higher courts may review or reverse his acts.
  • Macalintal v. PET (2011): Justices sitting in the Presidential Electoral Tribunal do not violate Sec. 12, because the PET exercises judicial power (see the PET section).
Trap: Sec. 12 bars designation to agencies with quasi-judicial or administrative functions; it does not bar judicial bodies created by the Constitution itself (the PET) or the Chief Justice’s constitutional seat as ex officio chair of the JBC.

Salary and tenure

Art. VIII, Sec. 10The salary of the Chief Justice and of the Associate Justices of the Supreme Court, and of judges of lower courts shall be fixed by law. During their continuance in office, their salary shall not be decreased.
Art. VIII, Sec. 11The Members of the Supreme Court and judges of lower courts shall hold office during good behavior until they reach the age of seventy years or become incapacitated to discharge the duties of their office. The Supreme Court en banc shall have the power to discipline judges of lower courts, or order their dismissal by a vote of a majority of the Members who actually took part in the deliberations on the issues in the case and voted thereon.
Art. VIII, Sec. 2 (second par.)No law shall be passed reorganizing the Judiciary when it undermines the security of tenure of its Members.

Salary and tenure are the classic guarantees of independence: a judge whose pay can be cut, or whose court can be abolished under him, decides with one eye on the other branches.

  • Nitafan v. Commissioner of Internal Revenue (1987): judges’ salaries are subject to the general income tax; that is not a “decrease.” The framers deliberately dropped the exemption read into the old text in Perfecto v. Meer and Endencia v. David. Congress may set a different salary: a higher one, or a lower one that applies only to judges appointed after the law.
  • Gacott (above) reads Sec. 11’s second sentence: the en banc is needed to dismiss a judge, not for lesser discipline.
Trap: “good behavior until seventy” covers lower-court judges too, but they are removed by the Supreme Court en banc, not by impeachment; only Members of the Supreme Court are impeachable (Art. XI, Sec. 2).

Removal

Art. XI, Sec. 2The President, the Vice-President, the Members of the Supreme Court, the Members of the Constitutional Commissions, and the Ombudsman may be removed from office on impeachment for, and conviction of, culpable violation of the Constitution, treason, bribery, graft and corruption, other high crimes, or betrayal of public trust. All other public officers and employees may be removed from office as provided by law, but not by impeachment.

Members of the Court are removable by impeachment. The question in these cases is what else may be filed against a sitting Justice. Two rules answer it: no proceeding may achieve removal by another route during incumbency (In re Gonzalez), but a defect in title, an ineligibility at appointment, may be raised by the State in quo warranto (Sereno).

  • In re Gonzalez (1988): Tanodbayan Gonzalez forwarded to Justice Fernan an anonymous letter repeating a dismissed disbarment complaint and asked him to comment. The Court, 13–0 per curiam, held that a Member of the Court, who must be a lawyer and is removable only by impeachment, cannot be disbarred during incumbency, nor charged criminally with an offense carrying removal. That would circumvent impeachment. It is not immunity but a procedural rule: impeach first, then prosecute. The rule covers other impeachable officers who must be lawyers, and a prosecutor must dismiss such charges motu proprio.
  • Republic v. Sereno (2018): “may be removed” in Art. XI, Sec. 2 is permissive; impeachment is not the exclusive mode. Quo warranto, filed by the State through the OSG, tests eligibility (title at appointment) and is judicial; impeachment tests fitness (acts in office) and is political. Sereno was ousted 8–6 on May 11, 2018. The June 19, 2018 Resolution denied her motion for reconsideration with finality, 8–6: imputing bias to the six Justices who testified in the House was no ground to inhibit; quo warranto by the State is imprescriptible; and the 90 days to fill the vacancy run from that Resolution. Dissents: Carpio, Leonen (“legal abomination”), Caguioa, Velasco, Jr., Perlas-Bernabe and Del Castillo, JJ. The impeachment side is on the other-powers map.
Gatmaytan (95 Phil. L.J. 529, 2022): he lists the 8–6 ouster of Sereno among the Duterte-era decisions that weakened checks on the President, quoting the view that the removal “through a highly dubious legal maneuver” effectively cowed the courts.
2026 news: VP Sara Duterte asked the Supreme Court in September 2026 to stop the grave-threats cases against her, claiming procedural immunity as an incumbent impeachable officer. In re Gonzalez is the case to test that claim against: its rule is about officers who must be lawyers and charges that would remove them. Ask whether either feature is present for a Vice-President charged with grave threats. The petition is pending; the story is on the Commander-in-Chief map.
Trap: In re Gonzalez bars disbarment and removal-carrying criminal charges during incumbency; it does not make a Justice immune, and it does not bar quo warranto on eligibility (Sereno). Quo warranto asks “was she ever qualified?”, not “has she misbehaved?”

Fiscal autonomy

Art. VIII, Sec. 3The Judiciary shall enjoy fiscal autonomy. Appropriations for the Judiciary may not be reduced by the legislature below the amount appropriated for the previous year and, after approval, shall be automatically and regularly released.

Fiscal autonomy is more than a budget floor and automatic release. It is the Judiciary’s full flexibility to allocate and use its resources, free from outside control, subject only to COA’s post-audit. Three lines of attack fail against it: cutting or withholding funds, attaching conditions to them, and an outside body dictating how the Court spends, charges or disposes of what is its own.

  • Bengzon v. Drilon (1992): the President’s veto of special provisions on retired Justices’ pensions in the 1992 GAA was void, 14–0: they were not items, and the veto impaired the Judiciary’s fiscal autonomy, “full flexibility to allocate and utilize” its resources. (The veto side is on the legislative process map.)
  • Re: COA Opinion on the Computation of the Appraised Value (2012): COA disallowed the Court’s formula for selling used vehicles and equipment to retiring Justices. The Court upheld its in-house computation, 13–0 per curiam: COA may post-audit the Court, but cannot impose its own formula on how the Court disposes of its property; that is the Chief Justice’s call, with the en banc.
  • Re: Perpetual Help Community Cooperative (2012): the Cooperative Code exempted cooperatives from court fees. The Court denied the exemption, 14–0. Legal fees are part of the rules of procedure, which only the Court promulgates (Art. VIII, Sec. 5(5)); and exemptions cut the funds (the JDF and SAJF) that secure fiscal autonomy.
RA 12233 (2025), Judiciary Fiscal Autonomy ActApproved August 14, 2025. The Supreme Court submits the Judiciary’s budget proposal to the DBM, and the Court’s original proposal is attached to the National Expenditure Program (Sec. 3). The Chief Justice may augment any Judiciary item from Judiciary savings and, by en banc resolution, modify allotments (Sec. 4(a)–(b)). The DBM automatically releases one-twelfth of the budget monthly, “without need for any request,” not conditioned on work and financial plans; the Court reports quarterly to the President and Congress (Sec. 4(c)). COA post-audits all expenditures and revenues (Sec. 4(d)). A Judiciary Trust Fund, administered by the Chief Justice, replaces the JDF (Sec. 5); the Court sets legal fees with access to justice in mind (Sec. 6), creates offices and positions within its resources (Secs. 7–8), and takes title to properties acquired for the Judiciary (Sec. 9).
Trap: fiscal autonomy does not exempt the Court from audit. COA keeps post-audit (Re: COA Opinion; RA 12233, Secs. 4(d) and 5); what it cannot do is pre-approve or substitute its judgment for the Court’s on how the Court uses its own resources.

Jurisdiction

Art. VIII, Sec. 2 (first par.)The Congress shall have the power to define, prescribe, and apportion the jurisdiction of the various courts but may not deprive the Supreme Court of its jurisdiction over cases enumerated in Section 5 hereof.
Art. VIII, Sec. 5(2)(a)[The Supreme Court shall have the power to] Review, revise, reverse, modify, or affirm on appeal or certiorari, as the law or the Rules of Court may provide, final judgments and orders of lower courts in: (a) All cases in which the constitutionality or validity of any treaty, international or executive agreement, law, presidential decree, proclamation, order, instruction, ordinance, or regulation is in question.
Art. VI, Sec. 30No law shall be passed increasing the appellate jurisdiction of the Supreme Court as provided in this Constitution without its advice and concurrence.

Congress apportions jurisdiction, with two limits: it cannot take away the Sec. 5 cases, and it cannot add to the Court’s appellate jurisdiction without the Court’s consent (Fabian v. Desierto, on the legislative process map). Jurisdiction also presupposes a legal right to enforce: courts do not sit to correct every mistake.

  • Felipe v. Leuterio (1952): a trial court recomputed the judges’ scores in an oratorical contest and changed the winner. The Court set it aside, 8–0: the judiciary has no power to reverse the award of the judges of an oratorical, literary or beauty contest. A contestant has no right to the prize until proclaimed; “error and wrong do not mean the same thing,” and the loss is damnum absque injuria. Fraud or malice would be a different case, against the erring judge.
  • Almora v. Dela Rosa (2018): in petitions against the PNP’s anti-drug circulars, the Court ordered the government to produce its reports on the drug-war operations and deaths. Cases on the constitutionality, “application or operation” of regulations (Sec. 4(2); Sec. 5(2)(a)) let the Court see how the circulars were carried out, to check the documents’ existence and facial compliance, not their truth. Routine police operations are not state secrets, and deaths in them get no presumption of regularity.
Trap: Felipe is not a political question case. The award is beyond the courts because no legal right was violated, not because another branch has the final say. Government-regulated contests (a civil service examination, a bar examination) are a different matter.

Deliberations

Art. VIII, Sec. 13The conclusions of the Supreme Court in any case submitted to it for decision en banc or in division shall be reached in consultation before the case is assigned to a Member for the writing of the opinion of the Court. A certification to this effect signed by the Chief Justice shall be issued and a copy thereof attached to the record of the case and served upon the parties. Any Member who took no part, or dissented, or abstained from a decision or resolution must state the reason therefor. All lower collegiate courts shall observe the same requirements.

A collegiate court decides as a body: consult first, then assign the writing. The certification, new in 1987, is the proof of it. But a missing certification does not void a decision, administrative cases and per curiam decisions need none, and minute resolutions need neither the Justices’ signatures nor the Chief Justice’s certification (LME 4.0, ch. 7). What the Justices said in deliberation stays inside the Court.

  • Prudential Bank v. Castro (1988): a disbarment respondent attacked a per curiam resolution for lacking the Sec. 13 certification. The certification applies to decisions in judicial cases, not administrative ones, where deliberation is inherent in a collegiate body; a per curiam decision has no ponente to assign; and the Justices’ signatures attest to the consultation anyway. A party cannot seek a Justice’s inhibition after the Justice has voted on the merits.
  • Consing v. Court of Appeals (1989): a CA decision without the certification is not void. Regularity is presumed; the omission only shows non-compliance and may make the responsible official answer for it.
  • In re Production of Court Records (2012): during Chief Justice Corona’s impeachment trial, the prosecutors subpoenaed the Court’s records and staff about its TRO in the Arroyo travel case. The Court, per curiam, held that its deliberations and predecisional records (raffle results, agenda, internal memoranda, drafts) are covered by judicial privilege: Justices and staff cannot be compelled to testify on internal adjudicative matters, only on external ones such as bribery. The privilege belongs to the Court; no single Justice may waive it. Public records may be produced as certified copies.
Trap: no certification ≠ no consultation (Consing). And the judicial privilege covers deliberative matters only; a Justice offered a bribe may be asked about the bribe.

Voting

Art. VIII, Sec. 4(2)All cases involving the constitutionality of a treaty, international or executive agreement, or law, which shall be heard by the Supreme Court en banc, and all other cases which under the Rules of Court are required to be heard en banc, including those involving the constitutionality, application, or operation of presidential decrees, proclamations, orders, instructions, ordinances, and other regulations, shall be decided with the concurrence of a majority of the Members who actually took part in the deliberations on the issues in the case and voted thereon.
Art. VIII, Sec. 4(3)Cases or matters heard by a division shall be decided or resolved with the concurrence of a majority of the Members who actually took part in the deliberations on the issues in the case and voted thereon, and in no case without the concurrence of at least three of such Members. When the required number is not obtained, the case shall be decided en banc: Provided, that no doctrine or principle of law laid down by the court in a decision rendered en banc or in division may be modified or reversed except by the court sitting en banc.

The base is always the Members who actually took part and voted, not the full fifteen. A Division needs that majority and at least three. When the en banc is equally divided, the Rules of Court decide the outcome: in an original action the petition is dismissed; on appeal the judgment below stands (Rule 56, Sec. 7). Nothing is declared unconstitutional by a tie.

  • Cruz v. Secretary of Environment and Natural Resources (2000): the challenge to IPRA split the Court 7–7 and stayed tied after redeliberation, so the petition was dismissed under Rule 56, Sec. 7. IPRA stands, but its validity was not decided; the separate opinions (Puno’s on native title) are not doctrine.
  • Gacott (above): a Division’s ruling by a majority of those who took part, and at least three, is the Court’s.
Trap: a 7–7 tie upholds nothing and strikes down nothing: the law survives only because the challenger failed to get a majority. Cite Cruz v. DENR for the effect of a tie, not for the validity of IPRA.

Requirements as to decisions

Art. VIII, Sec. 14No decision shall be rendered by any court without expressing therein clearly and distinctly the facts and the law on which it is based. No petition for review or motion for reconsideration of a decision of the court shall be refused due course or denied without stating the legal basis therefor.

Why the rule (LME 4.0, ch. 7, quoting Yao): the losing party is entitled to know why he lost, so he can point out the errors on appeal; and the rule assures the parties that the judge decided “through the processes of legal reasoning,” not ipse dixit. A judge, with “neither the sword nor the purse,” depends on reason for public confidence. The Rules repeat it (Rule 36, Sec. 1; Rule 120, Sec. 2: personally prepared, in writing, signed). Findings of fact mean the evidentiary facts, not conclusions. A verbal judgment is no judgment (Sebastian v. Reyes, 2009, a judge dismissed).

What satisfies Sec. 14. Facts adopted from a party’s brief (Hernandez); a memorandum decision that meets the Permskul conditions (Yao). What fails. A summary of testimony plus “credible” (Bugarin); a one-page affirmance with “no cogent reason” (Yao); a labor tribunal that never found who the employer was (Miguel). What is outside it. Minute resolutions refusing due course, which need only a legal basis (Nuñal), and administrative cases (Prudential). The Court disposes of most petitions by minute resolution where the case is patently without merit, the issues are factual, the decision below is supported by substantial evidence, or the petition only delays execution (Borromeo v. CA, 1990, in LME).

  • Miguel v. JCT Group, Inc. (2005): the duty reaches quasi-judicial bodies. A labor arbiter and the NLRC held two respondents liable without ever finding who employed the workers: grave abuse. The patently void decision was remanded, and the appeal-bond and reconsideration rules relaxed.
  • Nuñal v. COA (1989): Sec. 14’s “facts and law” applies to decisions on the merits in cases submitted for decision, not to a minute resolution refusing due course to a certiorari petition, which needs only its legal basis.
  • People v. Bugarin (1997): a rape conviction that only summarized the testimony and called the complainant credible “disrespects the judicial function.” Rather than remand a long-pending case, the Court reviewed the whole record itself.
  • Hernandez v. Court of Appeals (1993): the CA may adopt the narration of facts in the Solicitor General’s brief instead of rewriting it; it still states the facts on which it rests.
  • Yao v. Court of Appeals (2000): a memorandum decision (allowed by BP 129, Sec. 40) is valid only if (1) it annexes the lower court’s findings and makes them part of it, (2) the decision adopted itself complies with Sec. 14, and (3) it is used in simple cases where the facts are accepted and there is no doctrinal complication (Francisco v. Permskul, 1989). The RTC’s “starkly hollow” affirmance failed even that: nullified and remanded.
Gatmaytan (LME 4.0, ch. 7): brevity “should not be used as a substitute for substance,” and a memorandum decision “should be sparingly used lest it become an addictive excuse for judicial sloth” (quoting Permskul). Velarde v. Social Justice Society (2004) lists the parts of a good decision: the statement of the case, the facts, the issues, a ruling on each issue, and the dispositive portion. The dispositive portion controls; the body is where the facts and the law must be.

Petition for review and motion for reconsideration

The second paragraph of Sec. 14 asks less: a petition for review or a motion for reconsideration may be refused or denied with only a legal basis. “Lack of merit,” “the issues are factual,” “no reversible error,” or “nothing new” is enough, because the facts and the law are already in the decision being reconsidered or reviewed.

  • Tichangco v. Enriquez (2004): a collegiate court complies when, after deliberation, it denies a motion because the questions are factual or already passed upon, or cites another legal basis. Not mentioning every fact a party thinks crucial does not void a decision that gives a detailed account of the facts.
  • Martinez v. Court of Appeals (2001): a one-paragraph resolution saying the motion raises nothing new and its points were already considered and passed upon states a legal basis.
  • Hernandez (above): “no cogent reason to reverse” is a legal basis for denying reconsideration.
Trap: “no cogent reason” is enough to deny a motion for reconsideration (Hernandez), but not to decide an appeal: an appellate decision that only says it (Yao) has no facts and no law.

Periods for deciding cases

Art. VIII, Sec. 15(1), (2) and (4)(1) All cases or matters filed after the effectivity of this Constitution must be decided or resolved within twenty-four months from date of submission for the Supreme Court, and, unless reduced by the Supreme Court, twelve months for all lower collegiate courts, and three months for all other lower courts. (2) A case or matter shall be deemed submitted for decision or resolution upon the filing of the last pleading, brief, or memorandum required by the Rules of Court or by the court itself. … (4) Despite the expiration of the applicable mandatory period, the court, without prejudice to such responsibility as may have been incurred in consequence thereof, shall decide or resolve the case or matter submitted thereto for determination, without further delay.

The clock starts at submission (the last required pleading, brief or memorandum). When it runs out, the Chief Justice or presiding judge certifies why (Sec. 15(3)), and the court must still decide (Sec. 15(4)): lapse does not divest jurisdiction, but it exposes the judge to discipline. Delay is gross inefficiency; the only safe course is an extension asked for and granted before the period expires. Speedy disposition is also a right of the parties (Art. III, Sec. 16).

  • Re: Problem of Delays in Cases Before the Sandiganbayan (2001): the Sandiganbayan, a special court with trial functions, must decide in three months (PD 1606, Sec. 6), not the twelve months for “lower collegiate courts,” which means only the Court of Appeals; motions for reconsideration in 30 days. The Presiding Justice was fined ₱20,000 and relieved as division chair; even one long-delayed case is intolerable, and a Presiding Justice who does not assign a ponente is deemed the ponente.
  • OCA v. Fuentes III (2013): trial judges decide cases, and resolve motions and incidents, within 90 days. A judge cannot set his own deadline; without an extension from the Court, failing to decide even one case on time is gross inefficiency. He was fined ₱40,000 (first offense in over 15 years).
Trap: the Sandiganbayan is collegiate but gets three months, not twelve (Re: Delays). And an extension must be requested and granted; a judge’s heavy docket, illness or travel is no defense after the fact (Fuentes).

The Presidential Electoral Tribunal

Art. VII, Sec. 4 (last par.)The Supreme Court, sitting en banc, shall be the sole judge of all contests relating to the election, returns, and qualifications of the President or Vice-President, and may promulgate its rules for the purpose.

The PET is the Court en banc under another name, created by statute in 1957 and given constitutional footing in 1987. It decides contests (after proclamation), not pre-proclamation disputes or COMELEC disqualification cases.

  • Macalintal v. Presidential Electoral Tribunal (2011): the PET is constitutional, 15–0. The grant of “sole judge” power implies the means to exercise it; the Constitution “constitutionalized what was statutory,” and the framers meant the Court to organize the Tribunal. It exercises judicial power, “independent, but not separate” from the Court, so its Justices are not designated to an agency in breach of Sec. 12. Biraogo (on the Truth Commission) does not apply.
  • Marcos, Jr. v. Robredo (PET, 2021): the protest against VP Robredo was dismissed 15–0. A protest must be specific; the pilot provinces (at most three) decide whether it goes on; no reasonable recovery means dismissal; the PET may annul results but cannot call special elections. The walk-through is on the Commander-in-Chief map.
Trap: the PET has no separate Members: it is the Supreme Court en banc. Its Justices act as the Court, so the bar on designating judges to quasi-judicial agencies does not apply (Macalintal v. PET).
