Appointment and clemency

Syllabus V.B.2(d)–(e): the power of appointment, from Sarmiento v. Mison (1987) to Gonzales III (2014), and executive clemency, from Barrioquinto (1949) to Trillanes v. Medialdea (2024). Finals coverage. On an appointment news card, ask: which group (does it need the Commission on Appointments?); what kind (regular, ad interim, acting); when (the election ban; a vacancy; acceptance); for how long (fixed terms); and who can remove. On a clemency card: what kind, is it allowed, what it does and does not do, and can it be undone.

The appointing power and the Commission on Appointments

Art. VII, Sec. 16The President shall nominate and, with the consent of the Commission on Appointments, appoint the heads of the executive departments, ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, or officers of the armed forces from the rank of colonel or naval captain, and other officers whose appointments are vested in him in this Constitution. He shall also appoint all other officers of the Government whose appointments are not otherwise provided for by law, and those whom he may be authorized by law to appoint. The Congress may, by law, vest the appointment of other officers lower in rank in the President alone, in the courts, or in the heads of departments, agencies, commissions, or boards.
The President shall have the power to make appointments during the recess of the Congress, whether voluntary or compulsory, but such appointments shall be effective only until disapproval by the Commission on Appointments or until the next adjournment of the Congress.

The power to appoint is essentially executive. Congress may prescribe qualifications for an office, but it cannot name the appointee, force the President to appoint a particular person, or add to the offices that need the Commission on Appointments (CA). Limits on the power are construed strictly against the legislature. The CA, though made up of members of Congress, exercises an executive function when it consents (Pimentel v. Ermita).

  • Sarmiento III v. Mison (1987): Sec. 16 names four groups, and only the first needs CA consent: (1) heads of executive departments, ambassadors, other public ministers and consuls, officers of the armed forces from colonel or naval captain, and officers whose appointments the Constitution vests in the President; (2) all other officers whose appointments are not otherwise provided for by law; (3) those the President may be authorized by law to appoint; (4) lower-ranked officers whose appointment Congress vests in the President alone. The framers chose a middle ground between 1935 (nearly everyone confirmed, with horse-trading) and 1973 (no check), and deleted “and bureaus,” so the Customs Commissioner needs no confirmation. 9–2; Cruz, J., dissenting: “like checking the bridesmaids but forgetting the bride.”
  • Calderon v. Carale (1992): Congress cannot add to the first group. A Labor Code amendment requiring CA confirmation of NLRC commissioners (group three) is void, because it amends Sec. 16 by statute; the remedy is a constitutional amendment.
  • Manalo v. Sistoza (1999): the same for senior police officers. The PNP is “national in scope and civilian in character” (Art. XVI, Sec. 6), not the armed forces, so police directors are not “officers of the armed forces”; RA 6975’s confirmation clauses are void and severable.
  • Soriano III v. Lista (2003): the Coast Guard, moved from the Navy to the DOTC, is no longer part of the AFP; its admirals and captains need no confirmation. The petitioner, with no direct injury and no tax-and-spend issue, also lacked standing.
  • Outside Sec. 16, the Constitution provides other routes that need no CA: Justices and judges from a JBC list (Art. VIII, Sec. 9), the Ombudsman and Deputies from a JBC list (Art. XI, Sec. 9), and the Vice-President as a Cabinet member (Art. VII, Sec. 3). Officers the Constitution itself vests in the President, such as the constitutional commissioners, need CA consent.
Gatmaytan (Government Structure, ch. 6, 2015): he teaches the topic through two classifications: by nature (permanent, removable only for cause; temporary or acting, removable without cause) and by manner (regular, made while Congress is in session; ad interim, made during a recess), adding that appointments needing no CA confirmation are, strictly, neither regular nor ad interim. He then lists the four groups and the cases that keep the first group closed (Calderon, Manalo, Soriano). The book predates the assigned P&S (2024).
Trap: a rank title is not the test. A police “director” or a Coast Guard “admiral” needs no CA; an army colonel does. And “other officers whose appointments are vested in him in this Constitution” means officers the Constitution itself names (constitutional commissioners, regular JBC members), not officers a statute lets the President appoint (NLRC, CHR: group three).

Regular, ad interim and acting appointments

  • Matibag v. Benipayo (2002): an ad interim appointment, made during a recess, is permanent: it takes effect at once and cannot be withdrawn once the appointee qualifies; it ends only on CA disapproval or the next adjournment of Congress. So it is not the temporary or acting appointment the Constitution bars for constitutional commissioners. A by-passed appointment may be renewed (the CA acts “only by action, never by omission”); a disapproved one may not. Renewal is not reappointment, because “a reappointment presupposes a previous confirmed appointment.”
  • Pimentel v. Ermita (2005): the President may appoint acting department secretaries without CA consent even while Congress is in session. A secretary is the President’s alter ego, so the temporary one must be the President’s choice, not automatically the undersecretary. The Administrative Code (Book III, Sec. 17) allows designating any competent person for at most one year, a limit that guards against circumventing the CA. Only the senators who sat on the CA had standing.
  • General v. Urro (2011): the power to appoint includes acting appointments unless the Constitution or a law specifically forbids it or the office is repugnant to it (as the COMELEC is: Nacionalista Party v. Bautista); staggered terms are no bar. An acting appointee serves at pleasure, for at most a year; his separation is expiration, not removal, so he has no clear right to sue in quo warranto.
Trap: “ad interim” describes the manner (made in the meantime, during a recess), not the nature: an ad interim appointee has a permanent appointment and security of tenure until the CA acts. An “acting” appointee has neither. The constitutional commissions forbid acting appointments and designations (Brillantes v. Yorac), but not ad interim ones (Matibag).

The election ban on appointments (“midnight appointments”)

Art. VII, Secs. 14 and 15Appointments extended by an Acting President shall remain effective, unless revoked by the elected President within ninety days from his assumption or reassumption of office.
Two months immediately before the next presidential elections and up to the end of his term, a President or Acting President shall not make appointments, except temporary appointments to executive positions when continued vacancies therein will prejudice public service or endanger public safety.

Sec. 15 is the Constitution’s answer to Aytona v. Castillo (1962), where President Garcia made 350 appointments on his last night in office. It turns Aytona’s “stratagem to beat the deadline” into a fixed period: inside it, appointments are void whatever the appointee’s merit or the President’s motive, save the one exception.

  • Velicaria-Garafil v. Office of the President (2015): an appointment is a process of four elements that must all concur: (1) authority to appoint and evidence of its exercise; (2) transmittal of the appointment paper, with evidence (release through the Malacañang Records Office is the clearest proof); (3) a vacant position; (4) receipt and acceptance by a qualified appointee. Papers dated before the March 11, 2010 cut-off but transmitted and accepted after it were void, and President Aquino’s EO 2 revoking them was valid. 8–6; Brion, J., would read “appointment” narrowly as the President’s act of issuance, with acceptance allowed later.
  • De Castro v. JBC (2010): the ban does not apply to the Judiciary. Art. VIII, Sec. 4(1)’s 90-day period is a mandate to the President, and the JBC’s deliberate screening removes the evils Sec. 15 targets; In re Valenzuela (1998) was reversed. The JBC must submit its list before the vacancy so the President keeps the full 90 days; only the President appoints the Chief Justice. Carpio Morales, J., dissented.
  • De Rama v. Court of Appeals (2001): Sec. 15 binds only the President or Acting President. A new mayor could not recall his predecessor’s 14 last-month appointments, which were complete, attested by the CSC and assumed; only the CSC may recall an appointment, on grounds in its rules. 9–6; Mendoza, J., dissenting, invoked Aytona and public trust.
  • General v. Urro (2011): the Court will not decide whether appointments are midnight appointments when the case can be resolved on another ground (lis mota): the petitioner, an expired acting appointee, had no cause of action.
2026 news: the JBC sent its shortlist for Chief Justice on October 29, 2026; the President announced the appointment of Justice Rodil Zalameda on November 5, a day before Chief Justice Gesmundo’s retirement, and he took his oath on November 6. No election ban applied (the next presidential election is in 2028), and De Castro expects the list before the vacancy. A fair follow-up in orals: Garafil’s third element requires a vacant position, so when was this appointment complete? Answer from the dates: the oath, the act of acceptance, came when the vacancy arose.
Trap: three different time rules. Sec. 15 (the President, two months before the presidential election to the end of the term; executive posts only after De Castro). Sec. 14 (an Acting President’s appointments may be revoked by the elected President within 90 days). And the Omnibus Election Code’s own ban on appointments and promotions 45 days before a regular election (EO 2, Sec. 1(c)), a statutory rule.

Second posts for appointees: designations and ex officio seats

The appointing power runs into Art. VII, Sec. 13 and Art. IX-B, Sec. 7(2), set out on the President map (prohibitions and the ex officio exception). Two later cases apply them to temporary designations and to constitutional commissioners.

  • Funa v. Agra (2013): Sec. 13 covers temporary designations too: “to hold an office” is to occupy and discharge it. Agra could not be Acting Secretary of Justice while Acting Solicitor General; neither post is ex officio to the other, and the two offices are incompatible anyway. As a de facto officer, his acts stand.
  • Funa v. Civil Service Commission (2014): constitutional commissioners fall under the general rule (Art. IX-B, Sec. 7(2), read with Art. IX-A, Sec. 2), not Sec. 13. The Administrative Code may seat the CSC Chairman on boards affecting government employees, but EO 864’s seats on the GSIS, PhilHealth, ECC and Pag-IBIG boards went beyond his primary functions, paid per diems, and put him under bodies the President controls, impairing the CSC’s independence.

Fixed terms in the constitutional offices

Art. IX-D, Sec. 1(2) (the same pattern in Arts. IX-B and IX-C)The Chairman and the Commissioners shall be appointed by the President with the consent of the Commission on Appointments for a term of seven years without reappointment. Of those first appointed, the Chairman shall hold office for seven years, one Commissioner for five years, and the other Commissioner for three years, without reappointment. Appointment to any vacancy shall be only for the unexpired portion of the term of the predecessor. In no case shall any Member be appointed or designated in a temporary or acting capacity.
Art. XI, Secs. 9 and 11The Ombudsman and his Deputies shall be appointed by the President from a list of at least six nominees prepared by the Judicial and Bar Council, and from a list of three nominees for every vacancy thereafter. Such appointments shall require no confirmation. All vacancies shall be filled within three months after they occur. … The Ombudsman and his Deputies shall serve for a term of seven years without reappointment. …
  • Gaminde v. COA (2000): the rotational plan needs the first terms to start on a common date, February 2, 1987, whatever the dates of appointment, and vacancies to be filled only for the unexpired balance; a belated appointment is counted against the appointee. A term (the period held by right) differs from tenure (the time actually served). Gaminde’s term ended February 2, 1999, as her appointment said, but she keeps her pay for the extra year as a de facto officer in good faith.
  • Matibag v. Benipayo (2002): Gaminde’s reckoning created three COMELEC vacancies on February 2, 2001; the ad interim appointees were given terms ending February 2, 2008, and renewing them did not stretch that term.
  • Funa v. Villar (2012): a promotion from Commissioner to Chairman is not a barred “reappointment” (a second appointment to the same office), but no one may serve more than seven years in all, and the President cannot split terms: a seat vacated by expiration needs a full seven-year appointment, other vacancies only the unexpired portion. Villar’s promotion to a chairmanship vacated by expiration was void under any term. Matibag’s “fourth situation” was abandoned.
  • Ifurung v. Carpio Morales (2018): the Ombudsman is different. The Office is not a constitutional commission (Art. XI, Sec. 10 equates them only in rank and salary), so every Ombudsman and Deputy, even one filling a vacancy from resignation, serves a full seven years from appointment (RA 6770, Sec. 8(3)).
Trap: commissioners count from the February 2 cycle and fill only the unexpired portion of a vacancy from death, resignation, disability or impeachment; the Ombudsman and Deputies count from their own appointment and always get seven years. Don’t carry Gaminde over to the Ombudsman (Ifurung), or Ifurung over to the commissions.

The power to remove

The power to remove is implied from the power to appoint: as a rule, officers the President appoints are removable by the President. The rule gives way in three places: impeachable officers (Art. XI, Sec. 2); offices the Constitution makes independent; and officers with security of tenure, who may be removed only for a cause provided by law and with due process (Art. IX-B, Sec. 2(3)).

  • Larin v. Executive Secretary (1997): a BIR Assistant Commissioner, a presidential appointee in the Career Executive Service, was under the President’s disciplining authority, but only for cause. His dismissal fell when the Court acquitted him on findings that his acts were lawful, since the administrative case rested on the conviction. The President may reorganize, but EO 132 showed badges of bad faith (offices abolished and recreated to do the same work; more positions), and Larin, a permanent officer, should have been preferred over an outsider.
  • Gonzales III v. Office of the President (2012; 2014): the President cannot discipline or remove a Deputy Ombudsman. In 2012 the challenge to RA 6770, Sec. 8(2) failed for want of a majority, but Gonzales’s dismissal over the Quirino Grandstand hostage case was reversed: betrayal of public trust needs bad faith and gravity comparable to the other impeachment grounds. On reconsideration in 2014, Sec. 8(2) was declared unconstitutional as to the Deputies (8–7), because the Ombudsman’s independence is “similar in degree and kind” to the constitutional commissions’; by a separate 8–7 vote, it stays valid as to the Special Prosecutor.
2026 news: in a decision released May 2, 2026, the Third Division affirmed the CA that the Office of the President had no disciplinary authority over Overall Deputy Ombudsman Melchor Arthur Carandang, voiding his 2018 dismissal and awarding back salaries and retirement benefits, relying on stare decisis (Gonzales III).
Trap: Gonzales III is two rulings, and both were close. Cite the 2014 Resolution for the rule (Deputies protected; Special Prosecutor not) and the 2012 Decision for the meaning of betrayal of public trust. And the Ombudsman herself is impeachable, so the President cannot remove her at all.

The clemency power and its limits

Art. VII, Sec. 19Except in cases of impeachment, or as otherwise provided in this Constitution, the President may grant reprieves, commutations, and pardons, and remit fines and forfeitures, after conviction by final judgment.
He shall also have the power to grant amnesty with the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of the Congress.
Art. IX-C, Sec. 5No pardon, amnesty, parole, or suspension of sentence for violation of election laws, rules, and regulations shall be granted by the President without the favorable recommendation of the Commission.

The forms: a reprieve postpones the execution of a sentence; a commutation reduces it to a lighter one; a pardon forgives the punishment, absolutely or on conditions; remission of fines and forfeitures cancels a money penalty; amnesty erases the offense itself for a class of political offenders. Only amnesty needs Congress.

  • Risos-Vidal v. COMELEC (2015): the President may not pardon in only three situations: (1) impeachment cases; (2) cases without a conviction by final judgment; and (3) election-law violations without the COMELEC’s favorable recommendation. Otherwise, “subject to the limitations imposed by the Constitution, the pardoning power cannot be restricted or controlled by legislative action.” The ConCom voted 34–4 to delete a clause that would have let Congress limit clemency for violations of corrupt-practices laws.
  • Cristobal v. Labrador (1940) first said it under the 1935 Constitution: the only limits are those in the Constitution, so Congress cannot shield its own disqualifications from a pardon.
  • Monsanto v. Factoran (1989) explains why “final judgment” matters: the 1981 amendment to the 1973 Constitution allowed pardons before final conviction; the 1987 Constitution restored the requirement. Under the 1973 rule, Monsanto, pardoned while her motion for reconsideration was pending, was deemed to have abandoned her appeal.
Gatmaytan (Government Structure, ch. 6, 2015): he restates the three limits from Risos-Vidal and adds a fourth, as argument: “it can be argued that any act of Congress by way of statute cannot operate to limit the pardoning power.” He also stresses the final-conviction rule as the cases apply it: a pardon granted while an appeal is pending is void (People v. Casido), so the agencies must require proof that the appeal was withdrawn before acting on an application, and accepting a pardon does not withdraw the appeal (People v. Salle). The book predates the assigned P&S (2024) and may be outdated.
Trap: “after conviction by final judgment” applies to pardons, commutations and reprieves, not to amnesty, which may come before charges are filed, during trial, or after conviction. And the impeachment exception means the President cannot undo a Senate judgment of removal and disqualification; the officer’s criminal conviction for the same acts can still be pardoned.

Pardon: what it does and what it does not

Two lines of cases meet here. The broad line (Ex parte Garland, followed in Cristobal) says an absolute pardon “blots out the crime” and makes the offender “a new man.” The modern line (Monsanto) says a pardon forgives but does not forget. Both are still cited; the safe statement is that an absolute pardon removes the legal consequences of the conviction, and nothing more.

  • Cristobal v. Labrador (1940): an absolute pardon removes all disabilities resulting from the conviction, including a disqualification from voting that a later Election Code attached to it, even if the sentence had already been served. Santos’s pardon was absolute as to civil and political rights and conditional only as to the kinds of public posts he could hold.
  • Monsanto v. Factoran (1989): “Pardon implies guilt. It does not erase the fact of the commission of the crime and the conviction thereof. It does not wash out the moral stain. It involves forgiveness and not forgetfulness.” It restored Monsanto’s eligibility for appointment, not her post: she must be reappointed, and the appointing power may weigh what she did. No backpay (a pardon looks forward); the civil indemnity survives (RPC Art. 36). Six Justices concurred only in the result, reading Art. 36 to require the pardon to restore the right to hold office expressly.
  • Risos-Vidal v. COMELEC (2015): RPC Arts. 36 and 41 (a pardon does not restore the right to hold office or to vote, or remit perpetual absolute disqualification, unless it says so expressly) are harmonized with the Constitution: they only clarify that pardoning the principal penalty does not remit the accessory penalties unless the President includes them. “He is hereby restored to his civil and political rights” is express enough, because the right to seek elective office is a political right. The whereas clause noting Estrada’s promise not to run again was not a condition: a preamble is not operative. 11–3; Leonen, J., dissenting with Sereno, C.J., and Carpio, J.: “By reading an ambiguity in favor of a convicted public officer, impunity is tolerated.”

Trap: do not cite Monsanto’s concurrences (Padilla and Feliciano, JJ.: the right to hold office must be restored “in printer’s ink”) as the rule; Risos-Vidal called them not controlling. But they, and Leonen, J.’s Risos-Vidal dissent, are the best materials for the other side of a news card about a pardoned politician.

Conditional pardon and its breach

A conditional pardon is a grant the convict must accept; once accepted, its conditions bind him. When the President believes a condition was broken, there are two roads:

  1. Executive: the Revised Administrative Code, Sec. 64(i) lets the President “authorize the arrest and recommitment of any such person who, in his judgment, shall fail to comply with the condition.” The convict serves the remitted part of the original sentence. No conviction for the new act is needed, and the President’s finding is not reviewable.
  2. Judicial: RPC Art. 159 makes violating a conditional pardon a crime (prision correccional, minimum period). It needs a charge, a trial, and conviction by final judgment.
  • Torres v. Gonzales (1987): Torres, pardoned on condition that he would “not again violate any of the penal laws,” was recommitted after being charged with 20 counts of estafa and convicted of sedition by a trial court, with nothing yet final. The Court, following Tesoro, Sales and Espuelas, held that the President chose Sec. 64(i), so no final conviction was needed; due process is not always judicial process, and Torres had his day in court on the original crime. Punishing the new crimes still needs final convictions. 10–2; Cruz, J., dissenting: “it is the conviction that makes him a criminal.”
Trap: the Executive’s finding of breach only revives the old sentence. If the government wants to punish the new act, or the violation of the pardon itself (Art. 159), the courts must convict first.

Amnesty: claiming it and revoking it

Amnesty is the one form of clemency that needs Congress: the President grants it with the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of Congress (Sec. 19, par. 2; the vote is on the other-powers map). It is a public act that courts notice, granted to classes of political offenders, and it looks backward: it “abolishes and puts into oblivion the offense itself” (Barrioquinto). Under RPC Art. 89 it totally extinguishes criminal liability; civil liability to private persons may survive.

  • Barrioquinto v. Fernandez (1949): the classic table of pardon versus amnesty, and the holding (since abandoned) that an accused need not admit the act to claim amnesty, because amnesty is public and cannot be waived. Tuason, J., dissented: “amnesty presupposes the commission of a crime.”
  • Vera v. People (1963): the dissent became the rule. Following People v. Llanita and People v. Guillermo (1950), Barrioquinto was “superseded and deemed overruled”: it is a “rank inconsistency” to seek forgiveness for an act one denies; invoking amnesty is a plea of confession and avoidance, and the accused must prove the proclamation’s conditions. In any case, a killing born of rivalry between guerrilla units was not in furtherance of the resistance.
  • Trillanes IV v. Medialdea (2024): President Duterte’s Proclamation No. 572 (2018), voiding the amnesty granted to Sen. Trillanes under President Aquino’s Proclamation No. 75 (2010), was void (15–0). (1) The power is shared, so revocation needs Congress’s concurrence; otherwise the concurrence in the grant would be futile, a “sword of Damocles” over grantees. (2) The proclamation violated due process (the DND grant had become final and immutable, and he was not heard), equal protection (all 277 grantees’ forms were missing, but only his amnesty was voided), the ex post facto clause (it stripped a vested protection against prosecution), and double jeopardy (a dismissal on the ground of amnesty bars a new prosecution even though the accused moved for it). It was not a bill of attainder, and its arrest clause was read as requiring a court warrant. (3) On admission: it is enough to admit the covered acts and that they were criminal; Trillanes’s form did. (4) Letting the DND committee process the applications was not an undue delegation: the proclamation granted the amnesty, and the Secretary acted as the President’s alter ego.
Trap: Barrioquinto is still good law for the pardon-versus-amnesty distinction (Trillanes cites it for amnesty erasing the offense), but not for its no-admission rule. Cite Vera for the admission rule and Trillanes for how much admission is enough.
The rule that revocation needs Congress is in the body of Trillanes; the fallo only declares Proclamation No. 572 void. If a news card asks whether a President can revoke an amnesty with Congress’s concurrence, the Court has not had that case: say that Trillanes implies it may, subject to the Bill of Rights grounds it applied.
