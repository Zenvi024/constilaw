Legislative process and investigations

Syllabus V.A.7(c) to V.A.8: the Supreme Court’s appellate jurisdiction, how a bill passes, the veto, effectivity, the question hour, and inquiries in aid of legislation. Finals coverage. Read the digests in the Digests tab; this map turns them into rules you can apply out loud.

Congress and the Supreme Court’s appellate jurisdiction (Art. VI, Sec. 30)

Art. VI, Sec. 30No law shall be passed increasing the appellate jurisdiction of the Supreme Court as provided in this Constitution without its advice and concurrence.

Sec. 30 protects the Court from having its docket enlarged by statute. Read it with Art. VIII, Sec. 5(5): only the Court promulgates rules of pleading, practice and procedure, so Congress also cannot dictate which remedies are available against whom.

  • Fabian v. Desierto (1998): Sec. 27 of RA 6770, allowing appeals by certiorari to the SC from the Ombudsman’s decisions in administrative disciplinary cases, added to the Court’s appellate jurisdiction without its advice and concurrence. Void. Those appeals go to the Court of Appeals under Rule 43.
  • Carpio-Morales v. Court of Appeals (2015): the second paragraph of Sec. 14 of RA 6770 (no appeal or remedy against an Ombudsman decision except to the SC on pure questions of law) is unconstitutional; the first paragraph (no court may enjoin an Ombudsman investigation) is ineffective until the Court adopts it in its rules. Congress cannot take away the CA’s power to issue injunctions incidental to its jurisdiction.
Trap: Sec. 30 is about increasing the SC’s appellate jurisdiction. Congress may define and apportion the jurisdiction of lower courts (Art. VIII, Sec. 2), but it may not deprive the SC of its jurisdiction under Sec. 5, nor add to it without consent.

Passing a bill: readings, urgency, the bicameral committee and the enrolled bill

Art. VI, Sec. 26(2)No bill passed by either House shall become a law unless it has passed three readings on separate days, and printed copies thereof in its final form have been distributed to its Members three days before its passage, except when the President certifies to the necessity of its immediate enactment to meet a public calamity or emergency. Upon the last reading of a bill, no amendment thereto shall be allowed, and the vote thereon shall be taken immediately thereafter, and the yeas and nays entered in the Journal.
What a certification of urgency does, and does not, dispense with
  1. Dispensed with: readings on separate days, and printed copies in final form distributed three days before passage.
  2. Not dispensed with: the three readings themselves; no amendment on the last reading; the immediate vote with the yeas and nays in the Journal.
  • Kida v. Senate (2011): RA 10153 (ARMM synchronization) was passed on the President’s certification. The certification dispenses with the separate-day readings and the three-day printing, not the three readings; the Court generally does not review its factual basis.
  • Tolentino v. Secretary of Finance (1994): only the revenue bill must originate in the House; the Senate may propose amendments, even a substitute. The bicameral conference committee may reconcile and even insert provisions germane to the subject. Under the enrolled bill doctrine, the Court will not go behind the enrolled copy to check compliance with internal rules.
  • Ang Nars Party-List v. Executive Secretary (2019): only a bill becomes a law. A joint resolution, even one passed like a bill, is not a law and cannot amend a statute (Joint Resolution No. 4 could not lower the nurses’ salary grade fixed by RA 9173).
  • Miller v. Mardo (1961): there is no lawmaking by congressional silence. A reorganization plan that takes effect unless Congress disapproves it does not become a statute.
Gatmaytan (Government Structure, ch. 5): the President’s certification exempts both Houses from the three separate readings requirement; the Court will not inquire into the internal rules of Congress once the enrolled bill is certified by its presiding officers.
The digests of Tolentino disagree on whether RA 7716 violated Sec. 26(2). The decision held that the procedural requirements were complied with, so follow the card.

The veto: whole bills, items, and inappropriate provisions

Art. VI, Sec. 27(1) Every bill passed by the Congress shall, before it becomes a law, be presented to the President. If he approves the same he shall sign it; otherwise, he shall veto it and return the same with his objections to the House where it originated … If, after such reconsideration, two-thirds of all the Members of such House shall agree to pass the bill, it shall be sent … to the other House … and if approved by two-thirds of all the Members of that House, it shall become a law. … The President shall communicate his veto of any bill to the House where it originated within thirty days after the date of receipt thereof; otherwise, it shall become a law as if he had signed it. (2) The President shall have the power to veto any particular item or items in an appropriation, revenue, or tariff bill, but the veto shall not affect the item or items to which he does not object.
Art. VI, Sec. 25(2)No provision or enactment shall be embraced in the general appropriations bill unless it relates specifically to some particular appropriation therein. Any such provision or enactment shall be limited in its operation to the appropriation to which it relates.
  • The general rule is all or nothing: the President vetoes the whole bill. The exception is the item veto, available only in appropriation, revenue and tariff bills.
  • An item is an indivisible sum of money dedicated to a stated purpose. The President may not veto part of an item, or a provision that is not an item (Bengzon v. Drilon, 1992).
  • An inappropriate provision, general legislation riding on the GAA, unrelated to a particular appropriation, is treated as an item and may be vetoed (Gonzales v. Macaraig, 1990).
  • A veto cannot be used to repeal an existing law, set aside a final ruling of the Court, or dictate how the Judiciary (or another fiscally autonomous body) uses funds already appropriated to it (Bengzon v. Drilon).
  • Override: two-thirds of all the Members of each House, voting separately, by yeas and nays entered in the Journal. Pocket veto: none; silence for 30 days makes the bill a law.
Gatmaytan: any provision that does not relate to a particular item, or extends beyond it, is an inappropriate provision that can be vetoed separately; so are unconstitutional provisions and provisions meant to amend other laws.

Effectivity of laws

The midterm map already holds the 15-day clock (No publication, no law). For the finals, add two points. First, Tañada v. Tuvera (1985, 1986): publication in full is a requirement of due process for all statutes, including those of local application, private laws, and IRRs that enforce or implement laws; “unless it is otherwise provided” changes only the date, never the publication. Second, EO 200 (June 18, 1987) amended Art. 2 of the Civil Code: publication in the Official Gazette or a newspaper of general circulation. The same requirement governs the rules of procedure for inquiries (Garcillano).

The question hour (Sec. 22) is not an inquiry (Sec. 21)

Art. VI, Sec. 22The heads of departments may upon their own initiative, with the consent of the President, or upon the request of either House, as the rules of each House shall provide, appear before and be heard by such House on any matter pertaining to their departments. Written questions shall be submitted to the President of the Senate or the Speaker of the House of Representatives at least three days before their scheduled appearance. Interpellations shall not be limited to written questions, but may cover matters related thereto. When the security of the State or the public interest so requires and the President so states in writing, the appearance shall be conducted in executive session.

Senate v. Ermita (2006): Sec. 22 serves Congress’s oversight function; department heads appear with the President’s consent, so appearance is discretionary. Sec. 21 serves legislation; appearance is compulsory, and the only way out is a valid claim of privilege. Only the President and the Justices of the Supreme Court are exempt from the power of inquiry. EO 464 was void insofar as it required all officials to secure the President’s consent before appearing in Sec. 21 inquiries.

Gatmaytan: the 1987 Constitution removed the mandatory question hour to fit separation of powers; that only makes Congress’s compulsory power of inquiry, exercised in aid of legislation, more important.

Inquiries in aid of legislation: the three requisites

Art. VI, Sec. 21The Senate or the House of Representatives or any of its respective committees may conduct inquiries in aid of legislation in accordance with its duly published rules of procedure. The rights of persons appearing in, or affected by, such inquiries shall be respected.
Three requisites, then two defenses
  1. In aid of legislation, judged by the speech or resolution behind it; not a trial of private persons for a crime (Bengzon v. Senate Blue Ribbon).
  2. Duly published rules, in each Congress, in the Official Gazette or a newspaper of general circulation, unless the rules say they continue until amended (Garcillano; Neri; Ong (2023)).
  3. Rights of persons appearing respected: self-incrimination invoked question by question; no custodial-investigation rights; due process before contempt (Ong v. Senate Tricomm; Ong (2023)).
  4. Defense: a valid claim of privilege (Senate v. Ermita; Neri; AKBAYAN v. Aquino).
  5. Defense: the witness is beyond compulsion: the President, SC Justices, and (by the President’s order) military officers, whose attendance Congress must get through the courts (Gudani v. Senga).
  • The power is co-extensive with the power to legislate and reaches executive officials and agencies Congress may regulate or abolish; no statute or executive order can exempt anyone without a constitutional basis (Sabio v. Gordon, striking the PCGG exemption in Sec. 4(b), EO 1).
  • A pending court case does not by itself bar an inquiry (Sabio); it does when the inquiry would only decide the guilt of the defendants in that case (Bengzon).
  • When the President attacks the inquiry’s legislative purpose (not privilege), the committee must first resolve the jurisdictional challenge under its own rules; going straight to the SC is premature (Senate v. Medialdea, 2022).
  • The Court may enjoin an inquiry under its expanded jurisdiction, but only on a showing of grave abuse or unconstitutionality; Congress cannot cite Court of Appeals Justices in contempt (Agcaoili v. Fariñas, 2018).
Gatmaytan: inquiries and court proceedings serve different purposes: courts settle controversies between adverse parties; inquiries gather information so Congress can legislate wisely, even if no law results. The mere filing of a complaint in court should not bar a legislative investigation.

Executive privilege

  • What it covers. Kinds of information, not categories of persons. Recognized kinds: presidential communications, deliberative process, diplomatic negotiations, military and state secrets, and informer’s identity (Senate v. Ermita).
  • Who claims it, and how. The President, or the Executive Secretary “by order of the President”, expressly and with a specific basis. A blanket claim, or one made by the official alone, does not work (Senate v. Ermita).
  • Presidential communications (Neri, 2008): presumptively privileged if (1) they relate to a quintessential and non-delegable power, (2) they were authored, or solicited and received, by the President or a close advisor in operational proximity, and (3) no adequate showing of need and unavailability elsewhere overcomes them. Against Congress, the committees must show the information is critical to legislation, not merely pertinent.
  • Diplomatic negotiations (AKBAYAN v. Aquino, 2008): offers exchanged in treaty talks remain privileged even after the treaty is published; the party seeking them, citizens or Congress, must show sufficient need.

Contempt, arrest and detention

  • Each House and its committees may punish for contempt and order the arrest of a witness who refuses to appear or testify; the power is inherent (Sabio; Ong (2023)). A committee’s contempt order needs a majority of all its members after deliberation (Senate Rules, Sec. 18; Neri).
  • Due process first: before citing a witness for testifying falsely or evasively, the committee must give the witness a chance to explain; inconsistent answers are not by themselves evasive (Ong (2023)).
  • How long: detention under inherent contempt lasts only until the inquiry ends, on the approval or disapproval of the committee report, or at the end of the Congress; longer punishment requires statutory contempt (Art. 150, RPC), tried in court (Balag v. Senate, 2018).
  • Remedies of the detained witness: habeas corpus (SC, CA and RTC have concurrent jurisdiction; the SC will not take a case already with the CA), certiorari or prohibition on a showing of grave abuse; not amparo, which is limited to extralegal killings and enforced disappearances (Agcaoili).
