Judicial review

Syllabus III: separation of powers; justification of judicial review; political questions; the four requisites; effects of a ruling. 30 cases. This is the procedural layer of almost every answer.

Basis: separation of powers and checks and balances

Art. VIII, Sec. 1The judicial power shall be vested in one Supreme Court and in such lower courts as may be established by law. Judicial power includes the duty of the courts of justice to settle actual controversies involving rights which are legally demandable and enforceable, and to determine whether or not there has been a grave abuse of discretion amounting to lack or excess of jurisdiction on the part of any branch or instrumentality of the Government.

Separation of powers: legislation belongs to Congress, execution to the President, settlement of controversies to the courts. It is violated when one branch (a) impermissibly interferes with another’s function, or (b) assumes a function that belongs to another (Gatmaytan’s book, citing Belgica). Examples: a legislative veto over IRRs (ABAKADA); legislators identifying projects after the budget passes (Belgica); an agency “reviewing” a Supreme Court decision (In re Laureta); a statute interpreting the Constitution (Endencia).

Angara v. Electoral Commission justifies judicial review: when the Court allocates constitutional boundaries it asserts no superiority but performs its duty, limited to actual cases and the lis mota, with a presumption of constitutionality. Demetria v. Alba: the Court does not interfere while a branch acts within its authority, but must when it exceeds it.

Gatmaytan: the second paragraph of Sec. 1 (“grave abuse of discretion”) was written because Marcos’s Court hid behind the political question doctrine and earned a reputation as a rubber stamp. Under the 1987 Constitution, “almost every political issue now reaches the Supreme Court.”

Political questions

A political question is one which, under the Constitution, is to be decided by the people in their sovereign capacity, or in regard to which full discretionary authority has been given to the legislative or executive branch; it concerns wisdom, not legality. Under the expanded definition, the doctrine is narrow.

Truly political or justiciable? (Francisco)
  1. Does the Constitution impose limits or conditions on the power exercised (a vote, a time bar, a plebiscite, a procedure)? If yes, whether they were observed is justiciable.
  2. If the matter is left wholly to a political branch’s discretion with no standard, it is truly political; the Court may still ask only whether there was grave abuse.
  • Justiciable: the one-year impeachment bar (Francisco); the plebiscite for converting a city (Miranda v. Aguirre); Congress acting as constituent assembly (Gonzales).
  • Political: choosing a project route on grounds of wisdom (Torrecampo); the factual basis of an urgency certification is generally not reviewed (Tolentino v. Sec. of Finance; Kida).

The requisites of judicial review

Four requisites
  1. An actual case or controversy (ripe, not moot).
  2. A proper party with standing (personal and substantial interest; direct injury).
  3. The constitutional question raised at the earliest opportunity.
  4. The constitutional question is the lis mota (necessary to decide the case).

The syllabus opens the requisites with two cases: KAPIT v. City of Manila (2026), where challenges to the no-contact apprehension ordinances were dismissed as moot and for lack of standing; and White Light (2009), which lists the exceptions to the direct-injury rule and applies third-party standing.

1. Actual case: ripeness

The challenged act must have been accomplished and must have a direct adverse effect on the petitioner. Courts do not give advisory opinions.

  • PACU: fear that a regulatory power may be abused is not an injury.
  • Montesclaros: a bill is not a law; no review before enactment.
  • Lozano v. Nograles: a House resolution calling for a constituent assembly is not ripe.
  • PHILCONSA v. Philippine Government (2016): peace agreements needing a law to take effect are not ripe; Congress is not bound by them.

1. Actual case: mootness

A case is moot when a supervening event leaves no practical relief. The Court may still decide it if any of the four exceptions applies.

Exceptions to mootness (David v. Macapagal-Arroyo; Sanlakas)
  1. Grave violation of the Constitution.
  2. Exceptional character and paramount public interest.
  3. The need to formulate controlling principles for the bench, the bar and the public.
  4. Capable of repetition yet evading review.
  • Atlas Fertilizer: mooted by a later ruling and an amendatory law.
  • Lacson v. Perez: lifting the state of rebellion mooted the case.
  • Sanlakas: moot, but decided as capable of repetition.
  • KAPIT (2026): the exceptions apply to the issuance under challenge, not to a successor measure not yet challenged; interest of motorists alone is not ‘paramount public interest’.
Gatmaytan on Pangilinan v. Cayetano (ICC withdrawal): the Court invented “variant technicalities”: it denied senators standing until the Senate itself acted, and called the case moot because the withdrawal had taken effect. He argues standing of individual legislators was settled, an institutional position should not be required (or a majority could block every challenge by sitting on a resolution), and the case was not moot (an invalid withdrawal could not take effect) and fit every exception.

2. Proper party (standing)

General rule (direct injury test): the petitioner suffered an actual or threatened injury from the challenged act, fairly traceable to it, and likely to be redressed by a favorable ruling (AIWA v. Romulo; Joya).

Third-party standing (White Light)
  1. The litigant suffered an injury-in-fact.
  2. The litigant has a close relation to the third party.
  3. Some hindrance prevents the third party from protecting its own interests.

Transcendental importance can relax standing. Factors used: the character of the funds or assets involved; a clear disregard of a constitutional or statutory prohibition; and the lack of another party with a more direct interest. In IBP v. Zamora, the IBP lacked standing but the Court ruled anyway. In AIWA it refused to relax the rule.

Gatmaytan on Oposa (‘The Illusion of Intergenerational Equity’): the famous recognition of standing for future generations was obiter, not needed to decide whether the petitioners had a cause of action. Resident Marine Mammals later made animal ‘standing’ unnecessary by pointing to citizen suits under the environmental rules.

3. Earliest opportunity

The constitutional issue must be raised at the first chance in the proceedings, in the pleadings before a competent court, not for the first time on appeal (Arceta). It does not mean suing as soon as a law is passed; and a party may challenge a regulation whenever it is enforced against him (Gatmaytan’s book). Falcis (2019): the mere passage of the Family Code created no actual case; filing directly in the Supreme Court without an injury and without a proper respondent failed.

4. Lis mota

The constitutional question must be unavoidable; if the case can be decided on another ground, the Court will use that ground (Arceta). This follows from the presumption of constitutionality.

Effects of a declaration of unconstitutionality

  • Orthodox view (Civil Code, Art. 7): an unconstitutional act confers no rights, imposes no duties, creates no office; it is as if never passed.
  • Operative fact doctrine: acts done in good-faith reliance before the declaration may be respected to avoid injustice. Applied in Araullo (DAP projects that could not be undone, but not to authors without findings of good faith) and in Macalintal (2023) (the October 2023 barangay elections proceeded). Refused where it would reward the beneficiary of the void law (Planters Products v. Fertiphil).
  • Partial unconstitutionality: the valid part stands if separable. A separability clause creates only a presumption.
Gatmaytan on separability: the Court has lately seemed to require a separability clause to save valid parts (it voided a whole tax ordinance for lacking one). He argues severability should not depend on the clause; that was the Court’s usual approach, and it is the US rule.

Remedies and forum (quick guide)

  • Certiorari or prohibition under Rule 65 using expanded jurisdiction, filed directly in the Supreme Court for issues of transcendental importance; otherwise mind the hierarchy of courts.
  • Declaratory relief in the RTC for a law not yet violated (Exec. Sec. v. CA; White Light).
  • Electoral disputes: COMELEC before the winner becomes a Member; the HRET or SET after proclamation, oath and assumption; the Supreme Court (as PET) for the President and Vice President.
  • Money claims against the State: COA first (Fetalvero).
In a news question: write the procedural paragraph in this order: (1) remedy and forum; (2) actual case (is it only a bill, a proposal, a draft IRR?); (3) standing (who is suing, and as what?); (4) mootness if events overtook the case. Then turn to the merits. Two or three lines.

A party-list representative and a citizens’ group file certiorari in the Supreme Court against a House bill (approved on second reading) that would cap party-list nominees’ age at 60. Should the Court rule on it?

Answer: No. A bill creates no rights and imposes no duties; ruling on it would be an advisory opinion (Montesclaros; In re JDF). The petition is premature. The representative’s interest as a legislator is not impaired by a bill that is still being debated in her own chamber; her remedy is in the House. The petition should be dismissed without prejudice to a challenge once the measure becomes law.

Last-minute checklist

  • Four requisites; ripeness (accomplished act plus direct adverse effect); four exceptions to mootness.
  • Standing table: citizen, taxpayer, voter, legislator, association or third party, government, environment; transcendental importance factors.
  • Political question narrowed by Art. VIII, Sec. 1; Francisco test (are there constitutional limits?).
  • Bill or resolution = not ripe (Montesclaros, Lozano, JDF, PHILCONSA 2016).
  • Gatmaytan: Pangilinan’s “variant technicalities”; Oposa obiter; separability should not need a clause.
  • KAPIT (2026): mootness by supervening ordinance; unregistered associations lack capacity; third-party standing needs identified members.
