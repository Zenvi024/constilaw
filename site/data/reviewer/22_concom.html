Constitutional Commissions

The last part of the syllabus: the three independent Constitutional Commissions of Article IX, the Civil Service Commission, the Commission on Elections and the Commission on Audit. First the common provisions (independence, terms, disqualifications, fiscal autonomy) and how a commission decides and is reviewed; then each commission’s core powers and the cases that test them. On a news card about a removal, a reorganization, an election or plebiscite date, or an audit, ask: which commission, which power (and is it exclusive?), who else may act (Congress, the President, the courts), and how its ruling reaches the Supreme Court.

Common provisions: independence, terms, disqualifications, fiscal autonomy

Art. IX-A, Secs. 1–3Section 1. The Constitutional Commissions, which shall be independent, are the Civil Service Commission, the Commission on Elections, and the Commission on Audit. Section 2. No member of a Constitutional Commission shall, during his tenure, hold any other office or employment. Neither shall he engage in the practice of any profession or in the active management or control of any business which in any way may be affected by the functions of his office, nor shall he be financially interested, directly or indirectly, in any contract with, or in any franchise or privilege granted by the Government, any of its subdivisions, agencies, or instrumentalities, including government-owned or controlled corporations or their subsidiaries. Section 3. The salary of the Chairman and the Commissioners shall be fixed by law and shall not be decreased during their tenure.
Art. IX-A, Secs. 4–6 and 8Section 4. The Constitutional Commissions shall appoint their officials and employees in accordance with law. Section 5. The Commission shall enjoy fiscal autonomy. Their approved annual appropriations shall be automatically and regularly released. Section 6. Each Commission en banc may promulgate its own rules concerning pleadings and practice before it or before any of its offices. Such rules, however, shall not diminish, increase, or modify substantive rights. … Section 8. Each Commission shall perform such other functions as may be provided by law.

The commissions are independent: not under any department, and not under the President’s control even when their functions are executive in nature. The Constitution builds that independence from several parts: fixed, staggered seven-year terms without reappointment; appointment by the President with CA consent and no temporary or acting appointments; salaries that cannot be cut during tenure; the bar on other offices and conflicting interests (Sec. 2); the power to appoint their own staff (Sec. 4) and to make their own procedural rules (Sec. 6); and fiscal autonomy with automatic release (Sec. 5). Each body’s composition and qualifications are in its own article (IX-B, IX-C, IX-D, Sec. 1).

  • Terms. The first terms all ran from February 2, 1987, and every later term follows that cycle; a late appointment eats into the term (Gaminde v. COA). A Commissioner may be promoted to Chairman, but not beyond seven years in all, and the President cannot split terms: a seat vacated by expiration takes a full seven years, other vacancies only the unexpired portion (Funa v. Villar). An ad interim appointment is permanent, so it is not the barred “temporary or acting” appointment (Matibag v. Benipayo). All three are on the appointment map.
  • Second posts. Commissioners are governed by Art. IX-A, Sec. 2 and Art. IX-B, Sec. 7(2), not by the Cabinet rule; an ex officio seat must flow from their primary functions and be unpaid. The CSC Chairman’s seats on the GSIS, PhilHealth, ECC and Pag-IBIG boards were void and impaired the CSC’s independence (Funa v. Civil Service Commission, on the appointment map).
  • Fiscal autonomy. Approved appropriations are released automatically and regularly, and the Chairman may use savings to augment an existing item when a law authorizes it. In Goh v. Bayron (2014, 12–0) the COMELEC suspended a recall for “no funds”; the Court held that the GAA item for “recall votes” was a valid line item, the admitted shortfall was an actual deficiency, and the Chairman could augment it from savings, so the recall had to proceed.
Gatmaytan (2015 book, on the President’s relationship with the Constitutional Commissions): the commissions “perform vital governmental functions, and they have to be protected from external influences and political pressures”; they were made constitutional bodies, “independent of and not under any department of the government,” and “certainly … not under the control of the President,” even when their functions are executive. A final determination by the Department of Finance cannot bind the COA or foreclose its review. (His 2015 book predates the assigned 2024 edition; check this against the new edition when it arrives.)
Trap: the commissions’ fiscal autonomy is weaker than the Judiciary’s. They get automatic and regular release (Sec. 5) but no floor against cuts; the Judiciary’s appropriation cannot go below last year’s (Art. VIII, Sec. 3; automatic release). And autonomy is not a blank check: the COMELEC in Goh could augment only an existing item, with a law authorizing it.
Trap: Art. IX-A, Sec. 2 bars commissioners from any other office during tenure, and from practicing any profession; Sec. 3 protects their salaries only during tenure. A commissioner who resigns to run for office or to take another post is no longer a commissioner, but the seat is then filled only for the unexpired portion (Gaminde).

How the commissions decide, and review by the Supreme Court

Art. IX-A, Sec. 7Each Commission shall decide by a majority vote of all its Members any case or matter brought before it within sixty days from the date of its submission for decision or resolution. A case or matter is deemed submitted for decision or resolution upon the filing of the last pleading, brief, or memorandum required by the rules of the Commission or by the Commission itself. Unless otherwise provided by this Constitution or by law, any decision, order, or ruling of each Commission may be brought to the Supreme Court on certiorari by the aggrieved party within thirty days from receipt of a copy thereof.
Art. IX-C, Sec. 3The Commission on Elections may sit en banc or in two divisions, and shall promulgate its rules of procedure in order to expedite disposition of election cases, including pre-proclamation controversies. All such election cases shall be heard and decided in division, provided that motions for reconsideration of decisions shall be decided by the Commission en banc.

Three rules follow. A majority of all the Members, not of those present: four votes for the seven-member COMELEC, two for the three-member CSC and COA. Divisions first for COMELEC election cases, with reconsideration by the en banc; a party who skips the motion for reconsideration and goes straight to the Court from a division ruling has gone too early. And review: Sec. 7 sends commission rulings to the Supreme Court on certiorari within 30 days “unless otherwise provided by this Constitution or by law.” COMELEC and COA rulings go up that way under Rule 64, limited to grave abuse of discretion, with factual findings supported by substantial evidence respected; CSC rulings, as the law now provides, go first to the Court of Appeals on a petition for review under Rule 43.

  • Legaspi v. COMELEC (2016 Resolution, 8–7): a division disqualified a mayor for vote-buying; on reconsideration the en banc voted 3–2 (one Commissioner not taking part, one seat vacant), then 3–2–2 on rehearing. The COMELEC, applying Mendoza v. COMELEC (2010), dismissed the case outright. The Court reversed itself and Mendoza: the movant must convince four Commissioners; short of four after rehearing, the motion is denied and the division’s ruling stands. A motion for reconsideration is an “incidental matter” under Rule 18, Sec. 6, not an original action to be dismissed. Otherwise two votes (with abstentions) could undo a division’s valid ruling.
Trap: in Legaspi the losing side at the division got two votes to reverse and three to deny, yet under Mendoza it won. Ask who carries the burden: the movant. No four votes, no reversal.
Trap: COMELEC’s jurisdiction over election contests (Art. IX-C, Sec. 2(2)) covers regional, provincial and city officials originally, and municipal and barangay officials on appeal; its decisions on municipal and barangay contests are “final, executory, and not appealable,” but certiorari to the Supreme Court for grave abuse remains.

The Civil Service Commission: coverage, merit, tenure, rule-making

Art. IX-B, Sec. 2(1) The civil service embraces all branches, subdivisions, instrumentalities, and agencies of the Government, including government-owned or controlled corporations with original charters. (2) Appointments in the civil service shall be made only according to merit and fitness to be determined, as far as practicable, and, except to positions which are policy-determining, primarily confidential, or highly technical, by competitive examination. (3) No officer or employee of the civil service shall be removed or suspended except for cause provided by law. (4) No officer or employee in the civil service shall engage, directly or indirectly, in any electioneering or partisan political campaign. (5) The right to self-organization shall not be denied to government employees. (6) Temporary employees of the Government shall be given such protection as may be provided by law.
Art. IX-B, Secs. 3 and 7Section 3. The Civil Service Commission, as the central personnel agency of the Government, shall establish a career service and adopt measures to promote morale, efficiency, integrity, responsiveness, progressiveness, and courtesy in the civil service. … Section 7. No elective official shall be eligible for appointment or designation in any capacity to any public office or position during his tenure. Unless otherwise allowed by law or by the primary functions of his position, no appointive official shall hold any other office or employment in the Government or any subdivision, agency or instrumentality thereof, including government-owned or controlled corporations or their subsidiaries.

Coverage. The civil service covers the whole government, including GOCCs with original charters; GOCCs incorporated under the general corporation law are outside it (their people fall under the Labor Code). Merit. Appointments follow merit and fitness, by competitive examination except for policy-determining, primarily confidential and highly technical positions. Tenure. Removal or suspension only “for cause provided by law,” which means a legal cause relating to the office, after due process. Rule-making. As the central personnel agency, the CSC makes rules, but only to carry out the laws Congress passes.

  • De los Santos v. Mallare (1950): the President named a new Baguio City Engineer “at pleasure,” under an old Revised Administrative Code provision. The incumbent stayed: tenure covers the unclassified as well as the classified service, and only policy-determining, primarily confidential or highly technical posts are terminable at will. A city engineer executes policy, needs no close intimacy, and is technical but not highly so. “For cause” means legal cause, never the appointing power’s pleasure; the old removal-at-pleasure clause was repealed when the Constitution took effect.
  • Cuevas v. Bacal (2000; the syllabus’s “Demaisip v. Bacal”): a CESO III appointed Chief Public Attorney (a Rank I post) was replaced and made a regional director (a Rank III post). Valid: in the career executive service, tenure attaches to rank, not position; one holding a post above her rank holds it temporarily (Achacoso v. Macaraig), and CES officers may be moved in the public interest without loss of rank or salary. Three Justices dissented.
  • Buklod ng Kawaning EIIB v. Zamora (2001, 14–0): President Estrada deactivated the EIIB and separated its employees, then created Task Force Aduana. Valid: a good-faith reorganization (for economy or efficiency) abolishes offices, and valid abolition is “neither removal nor separation”; it is void if political or meant to defeat tenure (Dario v. Mison; RA 6656’s signs of bad faith). The President may reorganize executive offices under statute (the GAAs; Sec. 31, Book III, EO 292).
  • TIDCORP v. Civil Service Commission (2013, 14–0): TIDCORP’s charter exempts it from the laws on compensation, position classification and qualification standards, while it “shall endeavor” to conform to them “as closely as possible.” The CSC disallowed an appointment because the title was not in the DBM index, as a CSC circular required. Reversed: the CSC’s rule-making power is constitutional and cannot be withdrawn, but it implements the law and cannot override a charter exemption; the CSC still enforces position classification at TIDCORP, on TIDCORP’s own terms.
  • Flores v. Drilon (1993, 12–0): RA 7227 said the Olongapo mayor “shall be appointed” SBMA chairman for its first year. Void: an elective official may not be appointed to any office during his tenure, with no exception but those the Constitution makes (the President and the economic planning agency, the VP in the Cabinet, a member of Congress in the JBC); the “allowed by law or primary functions” exception is for appointive officials only. And Congress cannot leave the President no choice: the power of choice is the heart of the appointing power. Mayor Gordon kept his mayoralty, and his acts as chairman stand (de facto officer).
Trap: a reorganization is not a magic word. It is valid only in good faith; RA 6656 lists the signs of bad faith (more positions than before, the same office recreated under another name, less qualified people hired, the same functions reclassified). Buklod passed because Task Force Aduana hired no one new and cost a fraction of the EIIB.
Trap: the elective/appointive split in Art. IX-B, Sec. 7 decides most “second post” questions. An elective official cannot even resign after the appointment to cure it; he must resign first (Flores). An appointive official may hold a second post if a law or his primary functions allow it, unless he is in the Cabinet, where Art. VII, Sec. 13’s stricter rule applies (Civil Liberties Union).

The Commission on Elections: elections, plebiscites and its own processes

Art. IX-C, Sec. 2 (excerpt)The Commission on Elections shall exercise the following powers and functions: (1) Enforce and administer all laws and regulations relative to the conduct of an election, plebiscite, initiative, referendum, and recall. (2) Exercise exclusive original jurisdiction over all contests relating to the elections, returns, and qualifications of all elective regional, provincial, and city officials, and appellate jurisdiction over all contests involving elective municipal officials decided by trial courts of general jurisdiction, or involving elective barangay officials decided by trial courts of limited jurisdiction. … (3) Decide, except those involving the right to vote, all questions affecting elections, including determination of the number and location of polling places, appointment of election officials and inspectors, and registration of voters. …

The COMELEC is the Constitution’s election manager: it administers elections, plebiscites, initiatives, referenda and recalls; decides election contests for regional, provincial and city posts; and decides all questions affecting elections except the right to vote (which goes to the courts). The Court reads its administrative powers broadly and intervenes only for grave abuse.

  • Sula v. COMELEC (2023, 12–0): Cotabato City voted in January 2019 to join the BARMM. The plebiscite was within the Bangsamoro Organic Law’s 150 days (counted from effectivity, after the last required publication), and in any case the COMELEC has residual power to reset plebiscite dates, even beyond the statutory period (Cagas v. COMELEC); it cannot be “paralyzed by the literal interpretations of a guiding law.” It also frames the ballot question: a contiguous area is asked only whether to join. Claims of fraud are factual and must be specific and proven.
  • Goh v. Bayron (2014): the COMELEC may not shelve a recall that the law requires it to hold by pleading lack of a specific appropriation when it has a line item and savings to augment it (see fiscal autonomy above).
  • Legaspi v. COMELEC (2016): the four-vote rule for reversing a division (see how the commissions decide).
2026 news: RA 12317 (signed March 25, 2026) reset the first BARMM parliamentary elections to September 14, 2026 after the Court voided the earlier districting laws, and the Court affirmed on July 31 its ruling voiding the Bangsamoro redistricting law. The elections were held on September 14 after four postponements, with an 84.34% turnout reported by the COMELEC. For a news card: Congress sets election dates in the organic act; the COMELEC administers the polls (Art. IX-C, Sec. 2(1)) and may adjust a schedule when the law cannot be followed (Sula; Cagas); its acts are reviewed only for grave abuse.
Trap: the COMELEC’s power to reset a date is residual and administrative; it does not let the COMELEC postpone elections at will or override a date Congress has validly fixed when the election can be held. Sula and Cagas involve schedules the law set but the circumstances made impracticable.

The Commission on Audit: audit jurisdiction and its limits

Art. IX-D, Sec. 2(1) The Commission on Audit shall have the power, authority, and duty to examine, audit, and settle all accounts pertaining to the revenue and receipts of, and expenditures or uses of funds and property, owned or held in trust by, or pertaining to, the Government, or any of its subdivisions, agencies, or instrumentalities, including government-owned or controlled corporations with original charters, and on a post-audit basis: (a) constitutional bodies, commissions and offices that have been granted fiscal autonomy under this Constitution; (b) autonomous state colleges and universities; (c) other government-owned or controlled corporations and their subsidiaries; and (d) such non-governmental entities receiving subsidy or equity, directly or indirectly, from or through the Government, which are required by law or the granting institution to submit to such audit as a condition of subsidy or equity. … (2) The Commission shall have exclusive authority, subject to the limitations in this Article, to define the scope of its audit and examination, establish the techniques and methods required therefor, and promulgate accounting and auditing rules and regulations, including those for the prevention and disallowance of irregular, unnecessary, excessive, extravagant, or unconscionable expenditures or uses of government funds and properties.
Art. IX-D, Sec. 3No law shall be passed exempting any entity of the Government or its subsidiaries in any guise whatever, or any investment of public funds, from the jurisdiction of the Commission on Audit.

The COA audits all government funds and property, GOCCs included (those without original charters and their subsidiaries on post-audit), and private entities only as far as they hold government money. Two layers of power matter. The power to examine and audit (Sec. 2(1)) is not exclusive. The power to define the scope and methods of its audit, make auditing rules, and disallow irregular, unnecessary, excessive, extravagant or unconscionable spending (Sec. 2(2)) is exclusive. No law may take any government entity or public fund out of its reach (Sec. 3).

  • DBP v. COA (2002, 15–0): DBP, under a World Bank loan condition and a Central Bank circular, hired a private external auditor, and the COA disallowed the fees. Reversed: “exclusive” qualifies Sec. 2(2), not 2(1), and the Constitutional Commission rejected a ban on private auditors. Concurrent private audits are allowed, but the COA’s audit is preponderant: its findings bind government agencies and officials unless the courts reverse them, and only the COA may allow or disallow expenditures.
  • Special Audit Team, COA v. CA (2013, 13–0): the COA created a special team to audit the GSIS, which ran to the CA for an injunction while its own complaint was pending before the COA. The CA erred: under Sec. 2(2) the COA may organize its own audit, special teams included; complaints about the audit go up the COA’s own ladder first (auditor, director, Commission Proper), and courts are cautious with independent commissions.
  • Funa v. Manila Economic and Cultural Office (2014, 15–0): MECO, the non-stock corporation handling relations with Taiwan, is not a GOCC (it has corporate form and public functions but no government ownership or control); it is a sui generis private entity. But the COA audits a non-governmental entity as to funds coming from or through the government: the verification fees MECO collects for DOLE and its consular fees are auditable.
Trap: “the COA’s power is exclusive” is half right. Who may look at the books is shared (private auditors, the BSP); who may set the audit rules and disallow is the COA alone (DBP). And a private body is not immune just because it is private: follow the government money (MECO).
Trap: the COA post-audits bodies with fiscal autonomy, the Judiciary included, but cannot substitute its judgment for theirs on how they use their own resources (Re: COA Opinion, on the Supreme Court map). Audit is not control.
