The SC’s administrative and rule-making powers

Syllabus V.C.2(p): the Supreme Court as administrator of the whole Judiciary and the bar. Supervision of lower courts and their personnel; temporary assignments, change of venue and court personnel; the rules on constitutional rights (the writs of amparo, habeas data and kalikasan, with RA 10353); rules on procedure and the limits of that power; admission to the bar and the discipline of lawyers; and the integrated bar. On a news card about a judge, a court employee or a lawyer, ask: who may discipline (the Court alone, or another body too), for what (administrative or criminal), and in what order. On a card about a killing, abduction, red-tagging or surveillance, ask which writ fits and what must be proved.

Supervision of lower courts and court personnel

Art. VIII, Sec. 6The Supreme Court shall have administrative supervision over all courts and the personnel thereof.
Art. VIII, Sec. 11 (second sentence)The Supreme Court en banc shall have the power to discipline judges of lower courts, or order their dismissal by a vote of a majority of the Members who actually took part in the deliberations on the issues in the case and voted thereon.

Supervision is exclusive: from the Presiding Justice of the Court of Appeals down to the lowest court clerk, only the Supreme Court oversees compliance with “all laws, rules and regulations” and takes administrative action. Another branch that steps in violates the separation of powers. Other bodies are not shut out altogether: the CSC may detect and charge, and the Ombudsman may prosecute crimes, but the Court goes first on anything administrative. The Court acts through the Office of the Court Administrator (OCA), and it disciplines lawyers too, as officers of the court.

  • Maceda v. Vasquez (1993): an RTC judge was charged before the Ombudsman with falsifying his certificates of service (certifying that he had decided all cases within 90 days). The Court held, 13–0, that the Ombudsman has jurisdiction over the crime, since one act may bring both administrative and criminal liability, but must defer and refer: where a criminal complaint against a judge or court employee arises from administrative duties, the Court first decides whether the respondent acted within those duties. The Ombudsman cannot compel the Court to produce its records or its personnel to testify.
  • CSC v. Andal (2009): a Sandiganbayan guard was dismissed by the CSC for having someone else take his civil service exam. The Court held, 13–0, that the CSC cannot try and decide an administrative case against court personnel, even one arising from an exam anomaly. It files the complaint with the OCA (Ampong v. CSC); the Court decides. Andal was not estopped, having denied the charge, stayed away from the hearing and raised jurisdiction on appeal.
  • In re Demetria (2001): a CA Justice went to the DOJ with a suspected drug queen’s allies and asked prosecutors to withdraw a motion to inhibit her judge. He was dismissed, 14–0, under Rule 2.04 of the Code of Judicial Conduct: no judge may influence “in any manner” a case pending elsewhere, and judges must avoid even the appearance of impropriety, as private individuals too. He need not have known the accused.
  • Re: Conrado M. Vasquez, Jr. (2008): the Court of Appeals’ Meralco–GSIS scandal (a PCGG chairman calling his brother, a Justice; a ₱10 million offer to yield a division chairmanship; a “transcript” of deliberations written from memory). On a panel of retired Justices’ report, the Court dismissed Justice Roxas, suspended Justice Sabio for two months, reprimanded Justice Reyes, admonished Justice Dimaranan-Vidal, and severely reprimanded Presiding Justice Vasquez for failing to act decisively.
  • In re Undated Letter of Louis C. Biraogo (2009): an unpromulgated ponencia in the Limkaichong cases was leaked to a litigant, who showed it at a press conference. The copy could only have come from the ponente’s office. Retired Justice Ruben Reyes was fined ₱500,000 from his retirement benefits and disqualified from government office, 12–0: retirement does not moot an administrative case; the confidentiality of deliberations protects judicial independence; and res ipsa loquitur allows discipline on circumstantial evidence without a formal charge. He was ordered to show cause why he should not be disciplined as a lawyer, since In re Gonzalez bars disbarment only during tenure.
  • In re Gadon (2023): a lawyer’s viral video hurling sexual expletives at a woman journalist led to his disbarment, 15–0. Disbarment is sui generis (an inquiry into fitness), so the Court may preventively suspend at once, before the answer, when the act speaks for itself; a lawyer “cannot divide his personality,” and the CPRA reaches scandalous conduct “whether in public or private life” and gender-based harassment, online included.
Trap: Maceda does not give judges immunity from the Ombudsman. The Ombudsman keeps jurisdiction over the crime; it only has to wait for the Court’s administrative determination when the charge arises from court duties. And Andal does not stop the CSC from detecting exam fraud by court employees; it stops the CSC from deciding the case.
Trap: the Court disciplines its own retired Members administratively (In re Biraogo) and lower-court judges at any time, but a sitting Member of the Court is removed only by impeachment, and cannot be disbarred while in office (In re Gonzalez, on the Supreme Court map).

Temporary assignments, change of venue and court personnel

Art. VIII, Sec. 5(3), (4) and (6)[The Supreme Court shall have the following powers:] (3) Assign temporarily judges of lower courts to other stations as public interest may require. Such temporary assignment shall not exceed six months without the consent of the judge concerned. (4) Order a change of venue or place of trial to avoid a miscarriage of justice. … (6) Appoint all officials and employees of the Judiciary in accordance with the Civil Service Law.

The power to change the venue was first written into the 1973 Constitution, adopting the Court’s earlier ruling in People v. Gutierrez (1970). Crimes are best tried where they were committed, so a transfer is not granted for whimsical or flimsy reasons; it is granted when there are serious and weighty reasons to believe the original court would not give a fair and impartial trial: danger to the accused, fear among the witnesses, or a judge tied to a party. Doubts are resolved in favor of the transfer, and the plea may be made administratively, by letter or motion, without a full petition. A change of venue moves the whole record to the new court, not just the hearing.

  • People v. Pilotin (1975): Vincent Crisologo, charged with illegal possession of firearms in Vigan, showed that his family’s political enemies had killed his supporters and that his father, a congressman, was shot dead at mass in the Vigan cathedral. Hostile sentiment against the accused at the place of trial justifies a transfer: it is “absurd to compel him to undergo trial in a place where his life would be imperilled.”
  • Mondiguing v. Abad (1975): accused in the ambush of the Ifugao governor sought a transfer, saying the judge favored the governor and their witnesses feared reprisals. The same judge had been disqualified from an electoral protest as the governor’s alleged political leader, and one co-accused had been killed. The case went to the Circuit Criminal Court in Baguio City: a fair and impartial trial was in serious doubt.
  • People v. Sola (1981): seven bodies were found on a mayor’s hacienda; the mayor and others got bail without a hearing, and witnesses feared for their lives if the trial stayed nearby. The question is “the effect [fear] has on the witnesses who will testify,” even if the danger looks “less than terrifying” to others. The case had been moved to Bacolod City with orders to protect the witnesses, and the bail orders were void for lack of a hearing.

Temporary assignments let the Court send judges where they are needed (a backlog, a vacancy, a judge on leave), for at most six months unless the judge consents; the limit protects the judge’s tenure in his station. Court personnel are appointed by the Supreme Court, under the Civil Service Law; that is why the CSC’s rules apply to them but the Court alone disciplines them (Andal).

Trap: the Constitution says “to avoid a miscarriage of justice,” not “for the convenience of the parties.” Pilotin, Mondiguing and Sola all involve danger to life or a doubtful fair trial. A wish to be tried nearer home, or in Manila, is not enough.

Rules on constitutional rights: amparo, habeas data, kalikasan (and RA 10353)

Art. VIII, Sec. 5(5)[The Supreme Court shall have the following powers:] Promulgate rules concerning the protection and enforcement of constitutional rights, pleading, practice, and procedure in all courts, the admission to the practice of law, the integrated bar, and legal assistance to the underprivileged. Such rules shall provide a simplified and inexpensive procedure for the speedy disposition of cases, shall be uniform for all courts of the same grade, and shall not diminish, increase, or modify substantive rights. Rules of procedure of special courts and quasi-judicial bodies shall remain effective unless disapproved by the Supreme Court.
Rule on the Writ of Amparo (A.M. No. 07-9-12-SC, 2007), Sec. 1The petition for a writ of amparo is a remedy available to any person whose right to life, liberty and security is violated or threatened with violation by an unlawful act or omission of a public official or employee, or of a private individual or entity. The writ shall cover extralegal killings and enforced disappearances or threats thereof.
Rule on the Writ of Habeas Data (A.M. No. 08-1-16-SC, 2008), Sec. 1The writ of habeas data is a remedy available to any person whose right to privacy in life, liberty or security is violated or threatened by an unlawful act or omission of a public official or employee, or of a private individual or entity engaged in the gathering, collecting or storing of data or information regarding the person, family, home and correspondence of the aggrieved party.
Rules of Procedure for Environmental Cases (A.M. No. 09-6-8-SC, 2010), Rule 7, Sec. 1The writ [of kalikasan] is a remedy available to a natural or juridical person, entity authorized by law, people’s organization, non-governmental organization, or any public interest group accredited by or registered with any government agency, on behalf of persons whose constitutional right to a balanced and healthful ecology is violated, or threatened with violation by an unlawful act or omission of a public official or employee, or private individual or entity, involving environmental damage of such magnitude as to prejudice the life, health or property of inhabitants in two or more cities or provinces.

The 1987 Constitution added “the protection and enforcement of constitutional rights” to the Court’s rule-making power. The Court used it after the 2007 summit on extrajudicial killings: amparo (2007), habeas data (2008) and kalikasan (2010). All three are summary and need only substantial evidence. They find responsibility and accountability, not criminal, civil or administrative liability, and they supplement other remedies rather than replace them. Two stages matter: the court issues the writ on a prima facie showing on the face of the petition (Sec. 6), then grants the privilege after the return and a summary hearing (Sec. 18). Public officials who answer must prove extraordinary diligence (Sec. 17); the presumption of regularity does not help them. The petition is read in its totality, and hearsay and circumstantial evidence count if consistent with admissible evidence (Razon v. Tagitis).

Amparo’s coverage is extralegal killings and enforced disappearances, or threats of them, and nothing else: not custody, property or employment disputes, and not detention for legislative contempt (Agcaoili). Enforced disappearance has four elements (Navia v. Pardico, now RA 10353): a deprivation of liberty; by agents of the State or with its authorization, support or acquiescence; followed by a refusal to acknowledge it or concealment of the person’s fate or whereabouts; placing the person outside the protection of the law.

  • Rodriguez v. Macapagal-Arroyo (2011): a peasant organizer was abducted and tortured in an army camp for 11 days. Amparo found the military and police officials responsible or accountable. Command responsibility may be used, but only to identify who is accountable and must act. Former President Arroyo was not immune, since immunity lasts only during tenure, but no substantial evidence showed her responsibility. No temporary protection order may issue after the writs have.
  • Bautista v. Dannug-Salucon (2018): a human rights lawyer was under military surveillance, and her paralegal was shot dead the evening he warned her. On the totality of the evidence, hearsay included, the threats were real; courts need not wait for a killing. Habeas data ordered her data disclosed and destroyed, and the AFP and PNP heads had to investigate with extraordinary diligence; ordering subordinates to check is not enough.
  • Deduro v. Vinoya (2023): an activist named in army slides as a CPP-NPA leader, and on posters as a terrorist, while others tagged with him were killed. Red-tagging, vilification, labelling and guilt by association are threats to life, liberty and security. The RTC erred in dismissing outright: the writ issues on a prima facie case; the privilege is decided after the return and hearing.
  • Caram v. Segui (2014): a mother who gave up her baby to the DSWD sought amparo to get him back from prospective adopters. Denied: amparo is not for custody, and the State never hid the child, so there was no enforced disappearance. Her remedy is a custody case or habeas corpus.
  • Agcaoili v. Fariñas (2018, issues 1–3): amparo does not lie for persons detained by the House for contempt, absent a killing, disappearance or threat; and the court that first takes a habeas corpus petition (there, the Court of Appeals) keeps it, even against the Supreme Court. Issue 4 (Congress cannot cite CA Justices in contempt) is on the legislative process map.
  • Water for All Refund Movement v. MWSS (2023): a writ of kalikasan against a combined drainage-sewerage system failed. The petitioner must prove the three elements (a violation of the right to a balanced and healthful ecology, an unlawful act or omission, and damage prejudicing life, health or property in two or more cities or provinces); the magnitude of the damage is indispensable. The precautionary principle helps when the causal link is uncertain, not when there is no evidence; and the DENR should have been approached first.
RA 10353 (2012), Anti-Enforced or Involuntary Disappearance ActApproved December 21, 2012. Defines enforced or involuntary disappearance (Sec. 3(b)) and makes the right against it non-derogable, even in war or emergencies (Sec. 4). An “order of battle” is no defense, and a subordinate may disobey it (Sec. 5). Detainees have an absolute right to communicate (Sec. 6); officials must certify a person’s presence or absence on inquiry (Sec. 8) and keep a detention register open to relatives and lawyers (Sec. 10). Writs of habeas corpus, amparo and habeas data get priority and immediate compliance (Sec. 12). The immediate commanding officer who knew or should have known and failed to prevent or investigate is liable as a principal (Sec. 14). Penalty: reclusion perpetua (Sec. 15). It is a continuing offense while the fate of the victim is concealed (Sec. 21), does not prescribe unless the victim surfaces alive (then 25 years) (Sec. 22), and is excluded from amnesty (Sec. 23).
Trap: amparo is not a general remedy for every rights violation. Ask first: is there an extralegal killing, an enforced disappearance, or a threat of either? Red-tagging and surveillance count as threats (Deduro; Bautista); a custody fight (Caram) and contempt detention (Agcaoili) do not.
Trap: “issue the writ” and “grant the privilege” are different stages with different standards. A trial court that dismisses outright, without a return, because the evidence is not yet substantial applies the wrong standard (Deduro).

Rules on pleading, practice and procedure

Under Sec. 5(5), the 1987 Constitution made the Court’s rule-making power exclusive: Congress may no longer repeal, alter or supplement the Rules of Court, as it could under the 1935 and 1973 Constitutions. The limits are in the text: the rules must be simplified, inexpensive and uniform for courts of the same grade, and must not diminish, increase or modify substantive rights. A law that regulates procedure, or exempts parties from court fees, intrudes (Re: PHCCI, on the Supreme Court map). But the power reaches only what Sec. 5(5) lists.

  • Pimentel v. Legal Education Board (2019): the power over admission to the practice of law does not extend to the study of law. Legal education is an executive function, under the State’s reasonable supervision (Art. XIV, Sec. 4(1)), so RA 7662 and the LEB stand. But LEB powers that reach lawyers and the bar are void: training members of the legal profession (Sec. 3(a)(2)), internship as a requirement for taking the bar (Sec. 7(g)), and mandatory continuing legal education for practicing lawyers (Secs. 2 and 7(h)). The PhiLSAT, as a pass-or-fail requirement for admission to any law school, violated academic freedom. Vote: 8 concurring, 5 concurring and dissenting; Leonen, J., would have struck down all of RA 7662.
Trap: Pimentel cuts both ways. It limits the Court (no jurisdiction over law schools) and limits Congress and the LEB (no power over who takes the bar or how lawyers are trained). Sort each LEB power by asking: does it touch law students and schools, or lawyers and the bar?

Admission to the practice of law, and the discipline of lawyers

The practice of law is a privilege, not a right, and a continuing one. One becomes a lawyer by passing the bar, taking the oath and signing the Roll of Attorneys. The power to discipline and disbar lawyers is an incident of the exclusive power to admit them; disciplinary proceedings are sui generis, an inquiry into fitness, not a civil or criminal case, so prescription, desistance and the complainant’s interest do not control. Good moral character is required at admission and throughout practice.

  • Zaldivar v. Sandiganbayan (1988; the syllabus’s “Zaldivar v. Gonzales”): Special Prosecutor Raul Gonzalez told the press that the Court ruled against him out of vengeance and under a Justice’s pressure, and that the rich get favorable action. He was suspended indefinitely. The disciplinary power is broader than contempt; the Court need not inhibit or refer the case to the IBP; and a lawyer’s criticism of the courts must be bona fide and within the bounds of decency.
  • Aguirre v. Rana (2003): a bar passer appeared and signed pleadings as counsel before a board of canvassers before his oath. Practice of law is any activity requiring legal knowledge and skill (Cayetano v. Monsod); he was allowed to take the oath but denied admission (signing the Roll) for practicing without a license.
  • Garrido v. Garrido (2010): a lawyer married three times, twice bigamously, all before he passed the bar; his third wife, also a lawyer, knew. Both disbarred: pre-admission conduct counts, prescription and desistance do not apply, and the conduct was grossly immoral.
  • In re Ong (2014): Sandiganbayan Justice Gregory Ong, linked to Janet Lim-Napoles in the pork barrel hearings, was dismissed for gross misconduct, dishonesty and impropriety: bribery was not proven, but his dealings with a litigant whose case he had decided, and his untruthful account, were, by substantial evidence.
  • Zoleta v. Sandiganbayan (2015): a convict argued that the Sandiganbayan decision was void because Justice Ong, who concurred, was not natural-born. Rejected: Kilosbayan v. Ermita never ruled that he was not; a court later declared him natural-born; and he was at least a de facto officer, whose acts bind the public.
Trap: passing the bar does not make one a lawyer; the oath and the Roll do (Aguirre). And admission does not settle moral fitness for good: it is only a presumption, which acts before admission can rebut (Garrido).

The integrated bar

The Integrated Bar of the Philippines is a State-organized bar to which every lawyer must belong and pay dues. The Court integrated the bar in 1973 under RA 6397, and the 1973 and 1987 Constitutions put the “integrated bar” in its rule-making power. The Court supervises the IBP, elections included: it may investigate, annul elections, suspend oaths and amend the By-Laws. IBP elections are “strictly non-political.”

  • In re Edillon (1978): a lawyer refused to pay IBP dues, calling compulsory membership a violation of freedom of association. His name was stricken from the Roll, 12–0. Integration is valid police power; a lawyer is already a member of the bar, and the only compulsion is a reasonable regulatory fee; the practice of law is a privilege, not property.
  • In re: 1989 IBP Elections (1989): vote-buying, free plane rides, government officials campaigning and five-star “war rooms” marred the IBP national elections. The Court suspended the winners’ oath, investigated through five Justices, and annulled the elections, 12–0.
  • Re: Atty. Nilo Divina (2024): a law dean sponsored trips to Balesin and Bali and gave gift certificates to IBP-Central Luzon officers. No By-Laws campaign violation, since no candidacy was shown and the electors had not been chosen; but the lavish gifts were simple misconduct under the CPRA, and the giver and six recipients were fined ₱100,000 each (12–3).
Trap: compulsory IBP membership is not compelled association in the constitutional sense: the lawyer need not attend or vote, only pay (Edillon). And a By-Laws election offense needs a link to an election; gifts without one may still be misconduct (Divina).
