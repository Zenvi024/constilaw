Other powers of Congress and impeachment

Syllabus V.A.9: the non-legislative powers of Congress (canvassing, the special election, presidential disability, martial law, amnesty, treaties, war, emergency powers, natural resources, amendment) and the power of impeachment, from Francisco (2003) to the 2026 rulings on VP Sara Duterte. Finals coverage. Most of these powers are numbers: who votes, how many, together or apart. Learn the votes first, then the cases.

The votes: who decides, how many, together or apart

Every power in this part comes with a vote. On a news card, the first thing to check is whether the right body acted by the right number. Three phrases matter: “of all the Members” (count every seat, not only those present); “voting separately” (each House must reach the number on its own); and “voting jointly” (the Senate’s 24 and the House’s 300-plus votes are pooled, so the House dominates).

Trap: a joint session is not a joint vote. War is declared in joint session but the Houses vote separately (Art. VI, Sec. 23(1)); martial law is revoked or extended by Congress voting jointly (Art. VII, Sec. 18). And under Padilla v. Congress, Congress need not meet jointly at all unless it is going to vote on revocation.

Canvassing, the special election, and presidential disability

Art. VII, Sec. 4 (pars. 4 and 5)The returns of every election for President and Vice-President, duly certified by the board of canvassers of each province or city, shall be transmitted to the Congress, directed to the President of the Senate. Upon receipt of the certificates of canvass, the President of the Senate shall, not later than thirty days after the day of the election, open all the certificates in the presence of the Senate and the House of Representatives in joint public session, and the Congress, upon determination of the authenticity and due execution thereof in the manner provided by law, canvass the votes. The person having the highest number of votes shall be proclaimed elected, but in case two or more shall have an equal and highest number of votes, one of them shall forthwith be chosen by the vote of a majority of all the Members of both Houses of the Congress, voting separately. The Congress shall promulgate its rules for the canvassing of the certificates.
  • Pimentel v. Joint Committee of Congress (2004): the adjournment sine die of the last regular session (June 11, 2004) did not end the Twelfth Congress, whose term ran to noon of June 30; Art. VI, Sec. 15 concerns sessions, not the term. Canvassing is a non-legislative function: the joint public session cannot adjourn sine die until it has canvassed and proclaimed, the National Board of Canvassers is not functus officio until it finishes, and it may reconvene without a call from the President. The Joint Committee to which it referred the preliminary canvass may finish and report.
  • Contests over the election, returns and qualifications of the President and Vice-President go to the Supreme Court en banc as the Presidential Electoral Tribunal (Art. VII, Sec. 4, last par.), not to Congress. Congress canvasses; it does not try election contests.
Art. VII, Sec. 10The Congress shall, at ten o’clock in the morning of the third day after the vacancy in the offices of the President and Vice-President occurs, convene in accordance with its rules without need of a call and within seven days enact a law calling for a special election to elect a President and a Vice-President to be held not earlier than forty-five days nor later than sixty days from the time of such call. The bill calling such special election shall be deemed certified under paragraph 2, Section 26, Article VI of this Constitution and shall become law upon its approval on third reading by the Congress. Appropriations for the special election shall be charged against any current appropriations and shall be exempt from the requirements of paragraph 4, Section 25, Article VI of this Constitution. The convening of the Congress cannot be suspended nor the special election postponed. No special election shall be called if the vacancy occurs within eighteen months before the date of the next presidential election.
Presidential disability (Art. VII, Sec. 11), in four steps
  1. The President’s own written declaration to the Senate President and the Speaker that he is unable to discharge his powers and duties: the Vice-President acts as President until the President transmits a written declaration to the contrary.
  2. Or a majority of all the Members of the Cabinet transmit such a declaration: the Vice-President immediately assumes the office as Acting President.
  3. If the President then declares that no inability exists, he reassumes office, unless a majority of all the Members of the Cabinet, within five days, again declare that he is unable. Then Congress decides.
  4. Congress convenes within 48 hours if not in session, without need of a call; if within 10 days (or 12 days after it is required to assemble, if not in session) it determines by two-thirds of both Houses, voting separately, that the President is unable, the Vice-President acts as President; otherwise the President continues.
Trap: Sec. 11 covers temporary inability. Permanent disability, death, removal or resignation is a vacancy under Sec. 8 (the Vice-President becomes President). A vacancy in the Vice-Presidency is filled under Sec. 9: the President nominates a Member of either House, who takes office on confirmation by a majority of all the Members of both Houses, voting separately.

The legislative check on martial law and the suspension of the writ

Art. VII, Sec. 18 (par. 1, in part)Within forty-eight hours from the proclamation of martial law or the suspension of the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, the President shall submit a report in person or in writing to the Congress. The Congress, voting jointly, by a vote of at least a majority of all its Members in regular or special session, may revoke such proclamation or suspension, which revocation shall not be set aside by the President. Upon the initiative of the President, the Congress may, in the same manner, extend such proclamation or suspension for a period to be determined by the Congress, if the invasion or rebellion shall persist and public safety requires it. The Congress, if not in session, shall, within twenty-four hours following such proclamation or suspension, convene in accordance with its rules without any need of a call.
  • Padilla v. Congress (2017): after Proclamation No. 216 (Mindanao, May 23, 2017), the Senate and the House each adopted resolutions of support, and the Senate voted down a motion to convene jointly. The Court refused to compel a joint session. Verba legis: “may revoke” is permissive, and “voting jointly” attaches only to revocation (and to extension “in the same manner”). The Constitution is silent on how Congress concurs or supports, so each House may act under its own rules (Art. VI, Sec. 16(3)). But if either House resolves to revoke, Congress must convene jointly to vote. The framers removed prior congressional concurrence (28–12) so the President could act fast, and adopted joint voting (24–13) to make revocation easier and avoid deadlock.
  • Leonen, J., dissenting on the doctrine (joined by Caguioa, J.): the power to revoke includes a duty to deliberate jointly, in one forum; separate deliberations make the joint vote a “mere ceremony”, force officials to brief twice, and can deadlock in two of the four possible combinations of votes.
  • The three checks on the Commander-in-Chief work separately: Congress (revoke or extend, any time, on any ground: wisdom included); the Supreme Court (sufficiency of the factual basis, on a citizen’s petition, decided within 30 days); and the 60-day limit. The Court’s review and the Commander-in-Chief clause are in Batch 7 (V.B.2(f)); the same Padilla card appears there.
Gatmaytan (“The Philippines’ Authoritarian Constitution”, 95 Phil. L.J. 529 (2022)): in every case on Duterte’s martial law, Padilla included, the Court sided with the administration, “erasing virtually every constitutional safeguard” the 1987 Constitution built against martial law. Use this when asked whether the legislative check still works.

Amnesty: the President proposes, a majority of all the Members of Congress concurs

Art. VII, Sec. 19 (par. 2)He shall also have the power to grant amnesty with the concurrence of a majority of all the Members of the Congress.
  • Amnesty is granted by proclamation of the President with the concurrence of Congress; it is a public act of which courts take judicial notice. It is granted to classes of persons or communities who may be guilty of political offenses, generally before or after prosecution and sometimes after conviction. It looks backward and abolishes the offense itself: the person stands before the law as though no offense was committed.
  • Pardon, by contrast, is a private act of the President that must be pleaded and proved; it is granted only after conviction by final judgment, looks forward, and forgives the punishment; it does not restore the right to hold office or to vote unless it says so, and never exempts the offender from civil indemnity.
  • Limits: no pardon in cases of impeachment (Sec. 19, par. 1); no pardon, amnesty, parole or suspension of sentence for violations of election laws without COMELEC’s favorable recommendation (Art. IX-C, Sec. 5). Clemency and pardon are in Batch 6 (V.B.2(e)).
Gatmaytan (Government Structure, ch. 6): the Constitution has two provisions on clemency (Art. VII, Sec. 19 and Art. IX-C, Sec. 5); it can be argued that no statute can limit the pardoning power beyond the limits the Constitution itself imposes.
The Constitution says “a majority of all the Members of the Congress” without saying “voting jointly” or “voting separately”. If a news card turns on that, say so, and argue from the text: where the framers wanted a joint vote they said so (Sec. 18).

Treaties and executive agreements

Art. VII, Sec. 21No treaty or international agreement shall be valid and effective unless concurred in by at least two-thirds of all the Members of the Senate.
Art. XVIII, Sec. 25After the expiration in 1991 of the Agreement between the Republic of the Philippines and the United States of America concerning Military Bases, foreign military bases, troops, or facilities shall not be allowed in the Philippines except under a treaty duly concurred in by the Senate and, when the Congress so requires, ratified by a majority of the votes cast by the people in a national referendum held for that purpose, and recognized as a treaty by the other contracting State.
  • Pimentel v. Executive Secretary (2005): the President ratifies; the Senate only concurs in the ratification. Signature authenticates; ratification binds. The President may refuse to submit a signed treaty (the Rome Statute) to the Senate, or refuse to ratify after concurrence; no mandamus. A Senator has standing to protect the Senate’s power to concur; citizens and advocacy groups did not.
  • Bayan Muna v. Romulo (2011): an exchange of notes is a valid executive agreement, binding without Senate concurrence. The form of an agreement depends on the parties’ intent; the Eastern Sea Trading list is not cast in stone, and only Art. XVIII, Sec. 25 dictates a treaty by subject. The RP–US Non-Surrender Agreement does not defeat the Rome Statute (the ICC is complementary; the Philippines was then only a signatory) and does not amend RA 9851 (Sec. 17 says the Philippines “may” surrender). Carpio, J., dissenting: an executive agreement cannot amend a statute.
  • Saguisag v. Ochoa (2016): Sec. 25 governs only the initial entry of foreign bases, troops or facilities. After a treaty allows entry (the VFA, read with the MDT), the President may implement it by executive agreement (EDCA), so long as it stays within the treaty, creates no new obligations, and obeys the Constitution and laws. Agreed Locations are not bases: the Philippines keeps ownership, sovereignty, jurisdiction and access.
Executive agreements after Saguisag: four rules
  1. An agreement that allows the initial entry of foreign military bases, troops or facilities must be a treaty (Sec. 25).
  2. Otherwise the President chooses the form, but an executive agreement must be traceable to an express or implied authorization (the Constitution, a treaty or a law).
  3. It must stay within the parameters of the treaty or law it implements: no new or expanded obligations.
  4. It is inferior to treaties and statutes and must be consistent with the Constitution and the laws.
2026 news: the Senate concurred in the ASEAN Extradition Treaty on September 14, 2026 (Resolution No. 69; all 20 senators present voted yes) and in the Philippines–France Status of Visiting Forces Agreement on October 8, 2026 (17–0). Both clear the bar because two-thirds of all 24 Senators is 16. Read with Saguisag: the France SOVFA had to go to the Senate because it is the agreement that first lets French troops in (Sec. 25); EDCA did not, because it only implemented the VFA.

Withdrawal from treaties

The Constitution says nothing about withdrawal. Pangilinan v. Cayetano (2021) laid down guidelines “as guide for future cases”, adapting Koh’s mirror principle (exit with the same approval as entry) and the Youngstown categories:

  1. The President has leeway to withdraw from a treaty bona fide deemed contrary to the Constitution or the laws (because of a court ruling, a later law, or the President’s own judgment, which is reviewable for grave abuse).
  2. The President cannot withdraw unilaterally from a treaty entered into pursuant to legislative intent in a prior law, or affirmed by a later law, unless that law is repealed.
  3. The President cannot withdraw unilaterally where the Senate’s concurrence was expressly conditioned on its concurrence in withdrawal (in that resolution, a later resolution, a law, a joint resolution, or a comprehensive Senate resolution).
  4. A statute prevails over a treaty in case of conflict, because a statute passes both chambers while the Senate only concurs; Lantion’s “a treaty may repeal a statute” is misplaced obiter.

Applied to the Rome Statute: Senate Resolution No. 546 (2011) had no withdrawal clause (17 other concurrence resolutions did); RA 9851 was an independent, broader exercise of legislative will; Resolution No. 289 was never adopted. But the Court did not rule on the merits: the withdrawal took effect on March 17, 2019 under Art. 127, before the petitions could matter, so the case was moot, and the senators’ suit was premature because the Senate as a body never asserted its prerogative.

Gatmaytan (“Variant Technicalities”, lecture, 2024): the Court invented two technicalities. (1) Standing: a legislator always had standing to protect the prerogatives of the office; tying it to an institutional act of the Senate lets a chamber dominated by the President’s allies block suits by sitting on a resolution; and transcendental importance dispenses with standing, so the Court contradicted itself by asking for direct injury. (2) Mootness: if the withdrawal was unconstitutional, nothing valid reached the ICC (like a disqualified candidate who wins), and in any case all four exceptions applied. The case was a win for Duterte: the duty to keep cooperating with the ICC comes from Art. 127 itself, not from the Court. In 2025 he added that there is no legal basis for saying that helping execute an ICC warrant violates the Constitution: the Philippines remains bound for acts committed while it was a party.
2026 news: the ICC confirmed all charges against former President Duterte (April 23, 2026, a day after its Appeals Chamber upheld jurisdiction), and unsealed a warrant against Sen. Ronald dela Rosa (May 11, 2026), whom the Senate placed under “protective custody”. Both turn on Art. 127(2) of the Rome Statute and on RA 9851, Sec. 17 (surrender is discretionary: Bayan Muna).

War, emergency powers, natural resources, and amendment

Art. VI, Sec. 23(1) The Congress, by a vote of two-thirds of both Houses in joint session assembled, voting separately, shall have the sole power to declare the existence of a state of war. (2) In times of war or other national emergency, the Congress may, by law, authorize the President, for a limited period and subject to such restrictions as it may prescribe, to exercise powers necessary and proper to carry out a declared national policy. Unless sooner withdrawn by resolution of the Congress, such powers shall cease upon the next adjournment thereof.
Emergency powers under Sec. 23(2): five conditions
  1. There is a war or other national emergency.
  2. Congress delegates by law.
  3. For a limited period.
  4. Subject to the restrictions Congress prescribes.
  5. To carry out a declared national policy; the powers end at the next adjournment unless withdrawn sooner by resolution.
  • The President alone may declare a state of national emergency, but exercising emergency powers, including taking over private businesses affected with public interest under Art. XII, Sec. 17, needs a law: “the State” there means Congress (David v. Macapagal-Arroyo, 2006).
  • Natural resources (Art. XII, Sec. 2): the President may enter into agreements with foreign-owned corporations for technical or financial assistance in the large-scale exploration, development and utilization of minerals, petroleum and other mineral oils, under the general terms and conditions provided by law, and must notify Congress of every such contract within 30 days of its execution.
  • Amendment (Art. XVII): Congress proposes amendments or revisions by three-fourths of all its Members, or calls a constitutional convention by two-thirds of all its Members (or, by a majority of all its Members, submits the question of calling one to the voters). The midterm map has the full diagram: three doors in, one door out.
Gatmaytan (Government Structure, ch. 5): the Constitution has three emergency provisions: the delegation in Art. VI, Sec. 23(2); the Commander-in-Chief powers in Art. VII, Sec. 18; and the takeover of businesses in Art. XII, Sec. 17. Unlike the 1935 text (power “to promulgate rules and regulations”), Sec. 23(2) lets Congress delegate legislative power itself, with two limits: it is temporary, and Congress may add restrictions. Withdrawal is by resolution, which does not need the President’s approval, so the President cannot resist losing the powers.

Impeachment: who, for what, and how

Art. XI, Sec. 2The President, the Vice-President, the Members of the Supreme Court, the Members of the Constitutional Commissions, and the Ombudsman may be removed from office on impeachment for, and conviction of, culpable violation of the Constitution, treason, bribery, graft and corruption, other high crimes, or betrayal of public trust. All other public officers and employees may be removed from office as provided by law, but not by impeachment.
Art. XI, Sec. 3(1) The House of Representatives shall have the exclusive power to initiate all cases of impeachment. (2) A verified complaint for impeachment may be filed by any Member of the House of Representatives or by any citizen upon a resolution or endorsement by any Member thereof, which shall be included in the Order of Business within ten session days, and referred to the proper Committee within three session days thereafter. The Committee, after hearing, and by a majority vote of all its Members, shall submit its report to the House within sixty session days from such referral, together with the corresponding resolution. The resolution shall be calendared for consideration by the House within ten session days from receipt thereof. (3) A vote of at least one-third of all the Members of the House shall be necessary either to affirm a favorable resolution with the Articles of Impeachment of the Committee, or override its contrary resolution. The vote of each Member shall be recorded. (4) In case the verified complaint or resolution of impeachment is filed by at least one-third of all the Members of the House, the same shall constitute the Articles of Impeachment, and trial by the Senate shall forthwith proceed. (5) No impeachment proceedings shall be initiated against the same official more than once within a period of one year. (6) The Senate shall have the sole power to try and decide all cases of impeachment. When sitting for that purpose, the Senators shall be on oath or affirmation. When the President of the Philippines is on trial, the Chief Justice of the Supreme Court shall preside, but shall not vote. No person shall be convicted without the concurrence of two-thirds of all the Members of the Senate. (7) Judgment in cases of impeachment shall not extend further than removal from office and disqualification to hold any office under the Republic of the Philippines, but the party convicted shall nevertheless be liable and subject to prosecution, trial, and punishment, according to law. (8) The Congress shall promulgate its rules on impeachment to effectively carry out the purpose of this section.
  • Two modes of initiation. First mode (Sec. 3(2)): a verified complaint by a Member, or by a citizen with a Member’s endorsement, goes through the Order of Business, the Committee on Justice (form, substance, grounds, hearing, probable cause) and the plenary (one-third to affirm). Second mode (Sec. 3(4)): a complaint or resolution filed by at least one-third of all the Members is itself the Articles of Impeachment and goes straight to the Senate.
  • Impeachable offenses. What counts as “betrayal of public trust” or “other high crimes” was held a truly political question in Francisco. Duterte v. House (2025) added that the grounds must concern acts in relation to the office during the current term; Gatmaytan says that statement is not binding because the issue was not raised (video, May 2026); the Torreon petition (2026) invoked it against charges for acts as Education Secretary.
  • Removal is not the only way out of office. Quo warranto lies against an impeachable officer whose title was defective from the start (Republic v. Sereno); see the comparison below.

Initiation and the one-year bar

  • Francisco v. House (2003): “initiate” means the filing of the complaint and its referral to the Committee on Justice. The House rules that deemed a proceeding initiated only when the Committee found it sufficient, the House voted, or one-third filed (Secs. 16 and 17, Rule V, 2001 Rules) were void: the House’s rule-making power (Sec. 3(8)) is only “to effectively carry out the purpose” of Sec. 3. The first complaint against CJ Davide (filed June 2, referred August 5, 2003) started the clock, so the second (October 23, 2003, by more than one-third) was barred.
  • Gutierrez v. House Committee on Justice (2011): Francisco reaffirmed. Two complaints referred to the Committee at the same time initiate one proceeding: the candle image, two matches lighting one flame. The bar is about time, not the number of complaints: one proceeding, one defense, per year. The House in plenary decides referral; it may refuse a later complaint once one has been referred. “Promulgate” in Sec. 3(8) does not mean “publish”.
  • Duterte v. House (Decision, July 25, 2025; Resolution, January 28, 2026): the House cannot sit on first-mode complaints. The Sec. 3(2) periods are mandatory and ministerial; the Secretary General and the Speaker have no discretion to hold complaints. Under the Resolution, a first-mode complaint is deemed initiated when (a) it is referred; (b) it is verified and endorsed but not included in the Order of Business within 10 session days, or not referred within 3 session days after; or (c) it is left unacted on when Congress adjourns sine die. A second-mode complaint is initiated on valid endorsement by one-third, each Member verifying it; it is barred if a pending first-mode complaint already lapsed. The first December 2024 complaint should have been in the Order of Business by January 14, 2025 and referred by January 21; it was not, so the fourth complaint (February 5, 2025) was barred.

Gatmaytan (videos, September 2025): the ruling is “inherently contradictory”: how can complaints that were never initiated be dismissed? Without saying so, it abandons Francisco and Gutierrez. Allies of an official can now file defective complaints, and if the House sits on them or dismisses them, the bar runs and the official is shielded for a year. The due-process requirements have no constitutional anchor: under the second mode the Constitution says trial “shall forthwith proceed”, and due process in a non-criminal case means the chance to be heard, which the respondent gets at the Senate trial. Courts usually apply a new rule only to future cases (Azcuna’s point), though applying it here is defensible because the trial had not begun. He sides with the Court on one point: unlike the US, our Constitution writes limits into impeachment, so the Court may step in to enforce them.

Judicial review of impeachment

  • Reviewable where the Constitution sets limits (Francisco): the manner of filing, the votes, the one-year bar. Nixon v. US does not apply here: the US Constitution gives the House and Senate “sole” powers without such limits, and US judicial review is only implied. The Court will not decide whether the charged acts are impeachable.
  • Not purely political (Duterte v. House): impeachment is legal, political and constitutional; the Bill of Rights applies throughout; the Court asks whether the process was followed, whether the grounds are enumerated and within term, and whether rights were respected, but it does not decide when, who or whether to remove.
  • Certiorari and prohibition lie against the Committee on Justice (Gutierrez); mandamus does not lie to make the Senate start a trial on a timetable, absent clearly established grave abuse (Generillo).
  • Mootness ends many cases. Corona’s petition died with his conviction, his acceptance of the verdict and his leaving office (Corona v. Senate, 2012). Generillo’s died because the Articles were void (2026). The challenges to the House committee’s 2026 hearings died when the House approved the Articles and sent them to the Senate (Torreon, dismissed August 5, 2026).
  • Mid-trial challenges. On September 30, 2026 the Court unanimously dismissed three petitions (by Victor Rodriguez, Berteni Causing, and Atty. Ernesto Francisco Jr., the petitioner in Francisco) against the impeachment court’s ruling on the two-thirds vote. The midterm news map treats it as a question of standing and ripeness: the news box.

The Senate trial

  • “Forthwith” (Generillo v. Senate, April 22, 2026): within a reasonable time under the circumstances; “trial” includes preparations (rules, budget, logistics). Sec. 3(4) fixes no period for the Senate, unlike Sec. 3(2) for the House, so the Senate decides when the actual trial starts, but it may not unduly delay. Past practice: Estrada’s impeachment court convened in 7 days; Corona’s in 1 day; the first Sara Duterte Articles waited 126 days (February 5 to June 10, 2025). In 2026 the Senate convened as an impeachment court four days after receiving the Articles.
  • The presiding officer. The Chief Justice presides only when the President is on trial, and does not vote. In June 2026 the Senate amended Rule II of its impeachment rules: the Senate President presides unless a majority of the members present elects another senator (published June 9, 2026).
  • The vote to convict is two-thirds of “all the Members of the Senate”. On September 23, 2026 the impeachment court ruled 13–1 that this counts only senator-judges legally and actually able to take part (20 at the time, so 14 votes), reversing the presiding officer’s July 6 ruling that 16 of 24 were needed. Text against it: the Constitution says “Members present” when it means it, and “all the Members” elsewhere.
  • The verdict is conviction or acquittal. On June 10, 2025, the Senate, newly convened as an impeachment court, returned the Articles to the House, asking it to show that the initiation complied with the Constitution and to say whether the 20th Congress still wished to pursue the case.
  • The prosecution may narrow the case: on October 13, 2026 the court allowed the House prosecutors to withdraw three of the four Articles against the VP, leaving Article 2 (unexplained wealth, SALN non-disclosure and business interests) for the verdict.
Gatmaytan (video, June 2025): the “remand” of the Articles has no basis in the Constitution. Once the Articles reach the Senate, trial must proceed; an impeachment court decides guilty or not guilty. Constitutional objections are for the respondent to raise at trial, and for the Supreme Court to settle, not for senator-judges, some of whom were “lawyering” for the Vice President, which is a ground for their inhibition. On Generillo (May 2026): “not good news”, because the Senate can now justify any delay as a reasonable time.

The judgment, its effects, and quo warranto

  • The judgment “shall not extend further than removal from office and disqualification” (Sec. 3(7)), but the convicted officer remains liable to prosecution, trial and punishment according to law. Impeachment is neither criminal nor administrative, so there is no double jeopardy and no res judicata between it and a later case.
  • Re: Letter of Mrs. Corona (2021): CJ Corona, convicted on Article II by 20 votes on May 29, 2012, never had his civil, criminal or administrative liability judicially established (the cases ended with his death). Impeachment “touches neither his person nor his property”, so he was deemed involuntarily retired, with benefits under RA 9946, and his widow received survivorship benefits. Forfeiture needs a separate judgment.
  • Republic v. Sereno (2018): impeachment is not the exclusive mode of removal. Quo warranto tests title (eligibility when appointed); impeachment tests fitness (acts in office). The SALN gaps went to her “proven integrity” at appointment, so the Court, not Congress, could oust her (8–6). Prescription does not run against the State.
  • Immunity: only the President is immune from suit during the term. In September 2026 the Vice President asked the Court to stop a grave-threats case on the theory that impeachable officers have limited procedural immunity while in office; Gatmaytan said in January 2025 that only the President has immunity, so a plunder case may be filed against a sitting Vice President.
Trap: “removal” in Re: Corona and “ouster” in Sereno are different things. Impeachment is political and removes an officer who validly held office; quo warranto is judicial and declares that the officer never validly held it (a de facto officer).
