Interpreting and amending the Constitution

Syllabus I–II: preliminary readings; principles of interpretation; adoption and amendment (Art. XVII). 17 cases.

Why the 1987 Constitution reads the way it does

Gatmaytan’s starting point (book, Chapter 1; ‘Constitutional Amendments as Suspect Projects’): the 1987 Constitution was written after Marcos to place legal obstacles to dictatorship. It expanded judicial review, limited martial law, and made the judiciary independent. It was also a compromise drafted by an elite commission that “hewed closely to the status quo.” Filipinos have never amended it, because every Charter change attempt has been seen as a politician’s project to stay in power.

Gatmaytan: Charter change proposals are “suspect projects”: they fail unless politicians regain public trust. In an essay on any Charter change story, one line on this context shows mastery.

The preliminary US readings (Obergefell, 303 Creative, Dobbs) show a court recognizing an unenumerated right, narrowing its effect, and then overruling a precedent by appeal to history and tradition. Use them only as background on how courts change constitutions.

Self-executing test (Manila Prince Hotel)
  1. The provision is complete in itself and operative without enabling legislation.
  2. The nature and extent of the right conferred, and the liability imposed, are fixed by the Constitution.
  3. Nothing indicates the subject is referred to Congress for action.

The judges’ salary line: a lesson in who interprets

  1. Perfecto v. Meer (1950): income tax on a judge’s salary diminished it (1935 Constitution).
  2. Endencia v. David (1953): Congress passed RA 590 declaring such taxation is not a diminution. Void: Congress cannot interpret the Constitution.
  3. Nitafan v. CIR (1987): under the 1987 Constitution the framers intended judges’ salaries to be taxable; Perfecto and Endencia discarded on that point.
In a news question: when a bill “clarifies” a constitutional term (e.g., defines “natural-born”, “initiate”, or “savings” for constitutional purposes) or responds to a Supreme Court ruling, cite Endencia: Congress makes laws; the Court interprets the Constitution.

Amendment and revision (Art. XVII)

Art. XVII, Secs. 1 to 4Section 1. Any amendment to, or revision of, this Constitution may be proposed by: (1) The Congress, upon a vote of three-fourths of all its Members; or (2) A constitutional convention.
Section 2. Amendments to this Constitution may likewise be directly proposed by the people through initiative upon a petition of at least twelve per centum of the total number of registered voters, of which every legislative district must be represented by at least three per centum of the registered voters therein. No amendment under this section shall be authorized within five years following the ratification of this Constitution nor oftener than once every five years thereafter. The Congress shall provide for the implementation of the exercise of this right.
Section 3. The Congress may, by a vote of two-thirds of all its Members, call a constitutional convention, or by a majority vote of all its Members, submit to the electorate the question of calling such a convention.
Section 4. Any amendment to, or revision of, this Constitution under Section 1 hereof shall be valid when ratified by a majority of the votes cast in a plebiscite which shall be held not earlier than sixty days nor later than ninety days after the approval of such amendment or revision. Any amendment under Section 2 hereof shall be valid when ratified by a majority of the votes cast in a plebiscite which shall be held not earlier than sixty days nor later than ninety days after the certification by the Commission on Elections of the sufficiency of the petition.

Amendment or revision? (Lambino)
  1. Quantitative: is the change so extensive that it alters the substantial entirety of the Constitution?
  2. Qualitative: does it change the basic plan of government (separation of powers, checks and balances; presidential to parliamentary; bicameral to unicameral)?
If either test is met, it is a revision and cannot be done by initiative.

Leading cases in order

  1. Gonzales v. COMELEC (1967): Congress as constituent assembly is subject to judicial review; the plebiscite may coincide with a general election.
  2. Tolentino v. COMELEC (1971): all proposals of one convention go to the people together; no piecemeal submission.
  3. Planas and Javellana (1973): the 1973 Constitution was not ratified as the 1935 Constitution required, yet the Court let it stand.
  4. Santiago v. COMELEC (1997): initiative on the Constitution needs an adequate law; RA 6735 is inadequate.
  5. Lambino v. COMELEC (2006): initiative is limited to amendments; the people must author and sign a petition containing the full text.
Gatmaytan on Javellana (his favorite case): the Court “looked the other way.” A majority found the 1973 Constitution not validly ratified (Citizens’ Assemblies, show of hands, minors voting, martial law), yet dismissed the petitions. In doing so, the Justices removed the judiciary from a position of institutional significance and lost public respect; by 1986 the Court was seen as subservient to Marcos. In ‘Changing Constitutions: Judicial Review and Redemption’, he reads Lambino (2006) as the Court’s redemption: it blocked a government-driven ‘people’s’ initiative despite pressure.
Exam traps: (1) The vote in Congress is three-fourths of all Members, but the Constitution does not say whether the two chambers vote jointly or separately; the question is open. Argue that bicameralism and the structure of Art. VI support separate voting, and note the contrary reading. (2) An initiative signature sheet without the full text is not a “petition” (Lambino). (3) Santiago’s ruling that RA 6735 is inadequate was not revisited in Lambino, so there is still no adequate enabling law for an initiative on the Constitution.

A group gathers signatures for a “people’s initiative” to let Congress amend the economic provisions by ordinary law (“unless otherwise provided by law”). Local officials distribute the signature sheets, which describe the change in one sentence. Valid?

Answer: No. Under Art. XVII, Sec. 2, the people must author and sign the proposal embodied in a petition shown to them in full (Lambino); a one-line summary on a signature sheet does not comply. There is also still no adequate implementing law for initiatives on the Constitution (Santiago v. COMELEC). Finally, if officials drive the campaign, it is not a people’s initiative, the point Gatmaytan stresses in reading Lambino. (Whether letting Congress change economic restrictions by law is an amendment or a revision is arguable; the two defects above are enough.)

In a news question: a House resolution “calling” for a constituent assembly is not ripe (Lozano v. Nograles). An agreement by the Executive promising constitutional change is grave abuse (North Cotabato). A plebiscite on one proposal ahead of others is barred (Tolentino).

Judicial review of interpretation questions

Francisco v. House (2003) is the anchor for both interpretation and justiciability: where the Constitution sets limits on a political branch (the one-year bar on impeachment), the Court must enforce them. “Initiation” means filing plus referral to the Committee on Justice. In re JDF (2015) is the counterpoint: the Court will not rule on a bill.

Last-minute checklist

  • Three modes, vote thresholds, 60 to 90 days, initiative for amendments only, 12% plus 3% per district, once every 5 years.
  • Lambino’s two tests (quantitative, qualitative) and two requisites (people author and sign; full text in the petition).
  • Santiago: RA 6735 inadequate for constitutional initiatives; still unfixed.
  • Proper submission: same day as election allowed (Gonzales); no piecemeal submission (Tolentino).
  • Javellana’s votes: 6 justiciable, 6 not validly ratified, 6 to dismiss. Gatmaytan: the Court looked the other way.
  • Endencia: Congress cannot interpret the Constitution. Nitafan: framers’ intent controls. Manila Prince: presumption of self-execution.
