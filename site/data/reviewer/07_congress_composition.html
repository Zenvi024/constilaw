Congress: composition, party-list, qualifications and elections

Syllabus V.A.1–2: Art. VI, Secs. 1 to 9; RA 7941; RA 6645 and RA 7166; 18 cases. Tested in 2024 (party-list proposals) and 2023 (adding qualifications through a ‘debate’ requirement).

The provisions

Art. VI, Secs. 3, 5(1)–(2), 6Section 3. No person shall be a Senator unless he is a natural-born citizen of the Philippines and, on the day of the election, is at least thirty-five years of age, able to read and write, a registered voter, and a resident of the Philippines for not less than two years immediately preceding the day of the election.
Section 5. (1) The House of Representatives shall be composed of not more than two hundred and fifty members, unless otherwise fixed by law, who shall be elected from legislative districts apportioned among the provinces, cities, and the Metropolitan Manila area in accordance with the number of their respective inhabitants, and on the basis of a uniform and progressive ratio, and those who, as provided by law, shall be elected through a party-list system of registered national, regional, and sectoral parties or organizations. (2) The party-list representatives shall constitute twenty per centum of the total number of representatives including those under the party list. For three consecutive terms after the ratification of this Constitution, one-half of the seats allocated to party-list representatives shall be filled, as provided by law, by selection or election from the labor, peasant, urban poor, indigenous cultural communities, women, youth, and such other sectors as may be provided by law, except the religious sector.
Section 6. No person shall be a Member of the House of Representatives unless he is a natural-born citizen of the Philippines and, on the day of the election, is at least twenty-five years of age, able to read and write, and, except the party-list representatives, a registered voter in the district in which he shall be elected, and a resident thereof for a period of not less than one year immediately preceding the day of the election.

Qualifications cannot be added by law

The qualifications in Secs. 3 and 6 are exclusive. Congress cannot add to them by statute, directly or indirectly. In Social Justice Society v. Dangerous Drugs Board (2008), cited in Gatmaytan’s book, mandatory drug testing of senatorial candidates was void because it added a qualification. The same reasoning applies to any law that ‘disqualifies’ candidates who do something (or fail to do something) not listed in the Constitution.

In a news question (2023 Q4: a law disqualifying candidates who skip COMELEC debates): for President, Vice President, Senators and Representatives, the qualifications are fixed by the Constitution; disqualifying a candidate for skipping debates adds a qualification and is void (SJS v. DDB; Endencia’s logic that Congress cannot alter the Constitution by statute). For local offices, the qualifications are statutory, so Congress has more room, but the condition must still be reasonable and not burden suffrage arbitrarily (Albano; Macalintal 2023). Also mention free speech: a candidate cannot be compelled to speak.

Residence means domicile (Aquino; Marcos). A sitting official who keeps performing his office elsewhere cannot claim a new domicile (Mangudadatu, 2025).

Update (2025): Mangudadatu abandoned the second-placer rule: when a winning candidate is disqualified, the next-ranked candidate is no longer proclaimed; the office is filled by succession.

Legislative districts

  • Districts: contiguous, compact and adjacent territory; every city of 250,000 and every province gets at least one representative; reapportionment within three years after each census (Sec. 5(3)–(4)).
  • The House may exceed 250 by law, including piecemeal laws creating districts (Mariano v. COMELEC; BANAT, cited in Gatmaytan’s book).
  • Only Congress can create districts, so only Congress can create provinces and cities; the ARMM assembly could not (Sema).

The party-list system

Party-list representatives are 20% of the House, ‘as provided by law’ (RA 7941). The evolution:

  1. Ang Bagong Bayani (2001): the system is for the marginalized and underrepresented.
  2. Atong Paglaum (2013): abandoned that reading; national, regional and sectoral parties may join; national and regional parties need not be marginalized (six parameters on its card).
  3. Later cases apply Atong Paglaum: no track record required of the group (Abang Lingkod); no exclusion on moral grounds (Ang Ladlad).
  4. Albano (2023): Congress may set nominee qualifications, but they must pass equal protection; the ban on losing candidates was void.
Seat allocation (BANAT, as refined)
  1. Rank groups by votes; divisor is total valid party-list votes, including votes for groups on the ballot later disqualified (Araro).
  2. Each group with at least 2% gets one guaranteed seat.
  3. Remaining seats: multiply each group’s percentage by the remaining seats; whole numbers are additional seats; then one seat each down the ranking until filled. No 2% threshold in this round (AKMA-PTM).
  4. Maximum of three seats per group.

In a news question (2024 Q1: COMELEC chair’s proposals to amend the party-list law): Congress may amend RA 7941 because Sec. 5(1)–(2) leaves the system ‘as provided by law’. But (1) it cannot exclude national and regional parties or limit the system to the marginalized if that reading of the Constitution stands (Atong Paglaum held the framers did not intend an exclusively sectoral system); (2) any restriction on nominees (e.g., must be members of the sector) must have a rational basis under equal protection (Albano); (3) COMELEC cannot impose these by resolution; and (4) a proposal is not ripe for review until enacted.

Elections and vacancies

  • Regular elections: second Monday of May unless otherwise provided by law. ‘Unless otherwise provided by law’ means a law fixing another date or delegating it to COMELEC; COMELEC cannot suspend an election on its own (Bañas-Nograles).
  • Vacancies (Sec. 9): a special election ‘may be called … in the manner prescribed by law’; the winner serves only the unexpired term. Senate: with the next regular election (Tolentino, 2004). House: COMELEC must call it 60 to 90 days after the vacancy (if at least one year remains) without waiting for a House resolution (Hagedorn, 2025).
