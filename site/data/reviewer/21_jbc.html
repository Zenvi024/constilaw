Lower courts, the JBC, and automatic release

Syllabus V.C.3–6: the lower courts (their qualifications, appointment and tenure, and how far Congress may abolish or restructure them); the Judicial and Bar Council (its composition, the Court’s supervision, and how it nominates); and the automatic release of the Judiciary’s appropriations. On a news card about a new court, a reorganization, a JBC shortlist or a Supreme Court vacancy, ask: who acts (Congress, the JBC, the President, the Court), within what limit (tenure, the list, the 90 days, the JBC’s own rules), and whether the Court may review it.

Lower courts: qualifications, appointment and tenure

Art. VIII, Sec. 2The Congress shall have the power to define, prescribe, and apportion the jurisdiction of the various courts but may not deprive the Supreme Court of its jurisdiction over cases enumerated in Section 5 hereof. No law shall be passed reorganizing the Judiciary when it undermines the security of tenure of its Members.
Art. VIII, Secs. 7(2) and 11 (first sentence)(2) The Congress shall prescribe the qualifications of judges of lower courts, but no person may be appointed judge thereof unless he is a citizen of the Philippines and a member of the Philippine Bar. … The Members of the Supreme Court and judges of lower courts shall hold office during good behavior until they reach the age of seventy years or become incapacitated to discharge the duties of their office.

Lower courts are creatures of statute: Congress creates them, fixes their jurisdiction and the qualifications of their judges (above the constitutional floor: citizenship and bar membership; natural-born citizenship for members of lower collegiate courts, Sec. 7(1)). Judges are appointed by the President from a JBC list, without CA confirmation, within 90 days from the submission of the list (Sec. 9). They hold office during good behavior until 70, and only the Supreme Court en banc disciplines or dismisses them (Sec. 11). The hard question is abolition: if Congress may create a court, may it abolish it and with it the judges’ tenure?

  • Ocampo v. Secretary of Justice (1955): RA 1186 abolished all positions of judges-at-large and cadastral judges (who had no fixed station and served where the Secretary of Justice sent them) while adding district judges and abolishing no court. Seven of eleven Justices thought it an indirect removal that violated tenure, but a statute could then be declared void only by two-thirds, so the law stood. The prevailing four: the power to abolish courts includes the lesser power to reduce or recategorize judges; a judge holds office only as long as the position lasts; “a removal implies that the office exists after the ouster.” They added a caveat: not where judges are singled out or the reorganization cloaks an “evil purpose.”
  • De La Llana v. Alba (1982): BP 129 abolished every lower court except the Sandiganbayan and the CTA and replaced them with the IAC, RTCs, MeTCs, MTCs and MCTCs, leaving about 1,180 incumbents to be reappointed or not. The Court dismissed the challenge: abolition in good faith “suffers from no infirmity,” and “there can be no tenure to a non-existent office,” so valid abolition is not removal. Good faith was shown by the committee study, the hearings and the docket crisis. A mere renaming is not abolition (Brillo v. Enage). Because abolition separates judges as removal would, the Court read BP 129 to require that it be consulted on the incumbents. Teehankee, J., dissented: the new courts were the old ones renamed, so incumbents should stay.

The 1987 Constitution answered both cases in its text: “No law shall be passed reorganizing the Judiciary when it undermines the security of tenure of its Members” (Sec. 2). Congress keeps the power to create, abolish and restructure lower courts, but a reorganization that is really a purge of sitting judges now runs into an express ban.

RA 11459 (2019), Judges-at-Large ActApproved August 30, 2019. Creates 100 positions of Regional Trial Judges-at-Large and 50 of Municipal Trial Judges-at-Large, with no permanent salas, “assigned by the Supreme Court as acting or assisting judges … as public interest may require”; the Court fixes the length of each assignment. The JBC recommends the appointees to the President and, after the temporary assignment, recommends the permanent sala to which each will be appointed. RTC judges (at-large included) must be natural-born, at least 35, with 10 years of practice or law-requiring public office; first-level judges, natural-born, at least 30, with 5 years. They have the rank, pay and benefits of RTC or MTCC judges.
Trap: RA 11459 revives the judges-at-large that RA 1186 abolished in Ocampo, but fixes the old flaw. Under the 1948 law, the Secretary of Justice moved them around (Padilla, J., in Ocampo, thought that alone was unconstitutional); now the Supreme Court assigns them, in line with its power to assign judges temporarily (Sec. 5(3)) and its administrative supervision (Sec. 6). And they are appointed like any judge: from a JBC list.
Trap: Ocampo is not authority that seven votes can strike down a law. It shows the opposite: the petition failed because seven of eleven was short of two-thirds. Today the Court en banc declares a law unconstitutional by a majority of the Members who actually took part and voted (Sec. 4(2)), so the vote count would come out differently, which is why the four-Justice reasoning, not the head count, is what matters.

The Judicial and Bar Council: composition and supervision

Art. VIII, Sec. 8(1) A Judicial and Bar Council is hereby created under the supervision of the Supreme Court composed of the Chief Justice as ex officio Chairman, the Secretary of Justice, and a representative of the Congress as ex officio Members, a representative of the Integrated Bar, a professor of law, a retired Member of the Supreme Court, and a representative of the private sector. (2) The regular Members of the Council shall be appointed by the President for a term of four years with the consent of the Commission on Appointments. … (3) The Clerk of the Supreme Court shall be the Secretary ex officio of the Council and shall keep a record of its proceedings. (4) The regular Members of the Council shall receive such emoluments as may be determined by the Supreme Court. The Supreme Court shall provide in its annual budget the appropriations for the Council. (5) The Council shall have the principal function of recommending appointees to the Judiciary. It may exercise such other functions and duties as the Supreme Court may assign to it.

The JBC was created to depoliticize judicial appointments. Under the 1935 Constitution the President appointed with the CA’s consent, and aspirants courted legislators; under the 1973 Constitution, as amended, the President appointed alone. Now the President may appoint only from the JBC’s list. Its seven members: three ex officio (the Chief Justice, the Secretary of Justice, a representative of Congress) and four regular members appointed by the President with CA consent for four years. Beyond judges, the JBC also nominates the Ombudsman and his Deputies (Art. XI, Sec. 9).

  • Chavez v. JBC (2012 Decision; 2013 Resolution, 9–3): since 1994 a Senator and a Representative had both sat, with half a vote each and later a full vote each. Unconstitutional: “a representative of the Congress” means one seat with one full vote; the JBC has seven members, not eight; splitting the vote is a circumvention. The Framers adjusted other provisions to the bicameral Congress (the tie-break in Art. VII, Sec. 4; the VP vacancy, Sec. 9; martial law, Sec. 18) but not this one, because in the JBC Congress acts as one body in a non-legislative role; and the Court cannot supply an omission even if inadvertent (casus omissus). The JBC’s past acts stand (operative fact).
  • Umali v. JBC (2017, 9–5): the Houses then took the seat in turns, six months each. Valid, and Chavez stands as stare decisis: any Senator or Representative may represent the whole Congress, a “constricted” arrangement but not an absurd one; more seats need a constitutional amendment. A legislator may sue without an enabling resolution when Congress’s prerogatives are at stake. Leonen, J., with four others, would have abandoned Chavez.
  • Dulay v. JBC (2012): after Chief Justice Corona’s removal, the JBC (with no Chief Justice to chair it) took applications for Chief Justice. Valid: a complete JBC is only preferable, and the 90-day period is mandatory; but the most senior Justice who is not an applicant sits and presides. The Chief Justice is one of the “Members of the Supreme Court” the President appoints (Sec. 9).

Supervision, not control. The Court supervises the JBC: it oversees whether the JBC follows its own rules and may order a step redone, but it does not write the JBC’s criteria or choose its nominees. The JBC is neither judicial nor quasi-judicial, yet its acts are reviewable by certiorari for grave abuse under the expanded judicial power (Sec. 1); mandamus does not lie to compel a nomination, which is discretionary (Jardeleza v. Sereno; Villanueva v. JBC; Umali).

Gatmaytan (with Cielo Magno, “Averting Diversity: A Review of Nominations and Appointments to the Philippine Supreme Court (1988–2008),” assigned under V.C.5; and his 2024 lecture “Elitism of SC Nominees”): the JBC answered the martial law era, when the President’s choices went unchecked and appointees were often only nominally qualified (classmates, fraternity brothers), with no public say. But two decades on, the Court remained “an elitist institution”: most appointees came from the Judiciary (mostly the Court of Appeals and the Sandiganbayan), most were men in their sixties, women were a small minority of nominees and appointees, and appointing Justices close to 70 meant rapid turnover. His point for orals: depoliticizing appointments did not by itself diversify the Court. (The article itself is not in the files; this summary follows his lecture and his 2015 book’s citation of it. See also the Supreme Court map.)
Trap: the Chief Justice chairs the JBC ex officio, and that is no violation of Sec. 12’s ban on non-judicial work, since the Constitution itself assigns it. But when the Chief Justice is the vacancy, or is an applicant, she does not preside; the senior non-applicant Justice does (Dulay).
Trap: Chavez is about Congress’s seat, not about whether the JBC may be eight. If a law added an eighth member (say, a representative of the Ombudsman), the reasoning still applies: the enumeration in Sec. 8(1) is fixed, and changes need an amendment (Umali).

Nominating: criteria, due process and the list

Art. VIII, Sec. 9The Members of the Supreme Court and judges of lower courts shall be appointed by the President from a list of at least three nominees prepared by the Judicial and Bar Council for every vacancy. Such appointments need no confirmation. For the lower courts, the President shall issue the appointments within ninety days from the submission of the list.

The Constitution gives the minimum qualifications (Sec. 7) and the list (Sec. 9) but not the selection process. The JBC fills that gap with its own rules, now the 2020 Revised Rules of the JBC (JBC No. 2020-01, amended by Resolution No. 03-2021). They require, among others, that sitting judges serve five years in their present rank before promotion (Rule 3, Sec. 3), evidence of integrity and probity, with anonymous complaints acted on only on probable cause (Rule 4), at least four votes to make the list for appellate posts, the Ombudsman and his Deputies, the Special Prosecutor and the LEB (Rule 8, Sec. 3), no more than seven nominees per vacancy, with further rounds until there are at least three (Rule 8, Sec. 4), and, for the Supreme Court, enough years left to serve (2.5 years for appellate justices, chairs of constitutional commissions, the Solicitor General and Cabinet secretaries; 5 years for others; not applied to sitting Justices applying for Chief Justice) and due weight to the Supreme Court’s own recommendees (Rule 8, Sec. 1). Public interviews are covered live under the JBC’s media guidelines (revised July 3, 2026).

  • Villanueva v. JBC (2015, 11–0): a first-level judge with a year on the bench was not shortlisted for the RTC because of the five-year policy. The JBC may set criteria beyond the constitutional minimum (“sufficient but not unbridled license”); five years of service is a reasonable classification that gauges “proven competence.” But a qualification standard affects applicants, so it must be published (Tañada); the Court ordered publication. Having the qualifications gives no right to be listed.
  • Jardeleza v. Sereno (2014): the Chief Justice invoked the JBC rule requiring unanimity when an applicant’s “integrity” is challenged, against the Solicitor General, over his legal strategy in the South China Sea arbitration, and added an affair and insider trading on the day itself. The Court held that integrity means moral character, not professional judgment, and that an applicant objected to is owed due process: notice of the charge and reasonable time to answer (JBC-010), though no trial-type hearing. With the invocation void, his four votes put him on the list; the JBC was told to revise its rules. Leonen, J., dissented.
  • Aguinaldo v. Aquino III (2016; Resolution, 2017): for six new Sandiganbayan seats the JBC sent six separate numbered lists; President Aquino appointed across them. Valid: nomination is a qualification, meaning only that the President cannot appoint a non-nominee. The JBC cannot cluster nominees for simultaneous vacancies in a collegiate court; the President may treat the lists as one, with at least three nominees per vacancy. Seniority follows the order of the commissions, which is the President’s call, so the JBC cannot number the seats.
  • Citizens Adaza v. President Aquino and JBC (2012): following Dulay, the JBC may nominate and the President appoint a Chief Justice; the 90-day duty to fill a Supreme Court vacancy is “imperative,” and failure is “a clear disobedience to the Constitution.” Pending petitions against the Senate’s impeachment jurisdiction could not hold up the appointment.

Two more rules complete the picture. The election ban on appointments (Art. VII, Sec. 15) does not reach the Judiciary: the 90-day mandate of Sec. 4(1) prevails, and the JBC may and should submit its list before the vacancy (De Castro v. JBC, on the appointment map). And the qualifications themselves are judicially reviewable: Republic v. Sereno held that the JBC’s nomination did not cure a lack of proven integrity.

2026 news: the JBC opened applications for Chief Justice on August 29, 2026, ahead of Chief Justice Gesmundo’s compulsory retirement at 70 on November 6, requiring SALNs (with explanations for unusual changes in net worth), bank-secrecy waivers and sample decisions or writings, and warning that false statements disqualify. On October 29 it sent five nominees for Chief Justice (Justices Leonen, Caguioa, Zalameda, Dimaampao and Marquez) and eleven for the seat Justice Lazaro-Javier would vacate on November 16, after public interviews. The President appointed Justice Rodil Zalameda the 28th Chief Justice on November 5, a day before the vacancy, so no acting Chief Justice was needed. For a news card: the list (Sec. 9), the 90 days from each vacancy (Sec. 4(1)), the age rule in the JBC’s own rules, De Castro on early lists, and whether an appointment made before the vacancy is valid (the vacancy question under Velicaria-Garafil).
Trap: “at least three nominees for every vacancy” is a floor for the JBC, not a ceiling on the President’s choice among nominees. Aguinaldo lets the President pick from the whole pool for simultaneous vacancies; it does not let the President pick someone outside every list.
Trap: Jardeleza did not strike down the unanimity rule; it voided its invocation for lack of due process and for stretching “integrity” to cover legal strategy. The 2020 Revised Rules in the files no longer contain a unanimity requirement.

Automatic release of the Judiciary’s appropriations

Art. VIII, Sec. 3The Judiciary shall enjoy fiscal autonomy. Appropriations for the Judiciary may not be reduced by the legislature below the amount appropriated for the previous year and, after approval, shall be automatically and regularly released.

Sec. 3 gives the Judiciary three guarantees: fiscal autonomy (full flexibility to allocate and use its resources, with the Chief Justice’s authority to realign savings), a no-reduction floor (not below the previous year’s appropriation), and automatic and regular release of what Congress has approved, without the executive’s say-so. The Constitutional Commissions get fiscal autonomy and automatic release too (Art. IX-A, Sec. 5), and so does the Ombudsman (Art. XI, Sec. 14), but only the Judiciary has the floor.

  • Bengzon v. Drilon (1992): Congress put special provisions in the 1992 GAA to fund the adjusted pensions of retired Justices (re-enacting a readjustment law the President had earlier vetoed), and President Aquino vetoed those provisions. Void, 14–0: they were not items, so they could not be vetoed alone; a veto cannot repeal an existing law; and the veto impaired the Judiciary’s fiscal autonomy, which “contemplates a guarantee of full flexibility to allocate and utilize [its] resources with the wisdom and dispatch that [its] needs require.” The vetoed provisions were declared valid and the funds ordered released automatically and regularly. (The item veto side of the case is on the legislative process map.)

RA 12233, the Judiciary Fiscal Autonomy Act (approved August 14, 2025), now spells this out in statute: the Court’s original budget proposal is attached to the National Expenditure Program; the Chief Justice may augment Judiciary items from Judiciary savings; and the DBM releases one-twelfth of the budget each month “without need for any request,” with COA keeping post-audit. See the Supreme Court map for RA 12233 and Re: COA Opinion (2012).

Trap: “automatically released” does not mean Congress must give the Judiciary what it asks for. Congress still decides the amount; it simply cannot go below last year’s, and once it approves, the DBM cannot withhold or condition the release. For the commissions, the same release rule applies but without the floor, and they spend within the items Congress gives them (Goh v. Bayron, on the Constitutional Commissions map).
