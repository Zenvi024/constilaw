Exam-room rules (Sir Dan, via the beadle, Oct. 13, 2026)

Breaking any of these means a 5.0 for the exam
  1. Identify yourself only by your student ID number. Do not identify yourself in any other manner.
  2. The questionnaire is your answer sheet. Write at the back of the questionnaire. All answers must fit in the back.
  3. “Do not write a dissertation.”
  4. No communication with classmates in any manner.
  5. No electronic devices. All you need is a pen (and your correction tape). Nothing else on your table.
  6. Answers that cannot be read are marked incorrect.
What this changes for you: (1) No yellow paper: everything goes on one side of the questionnaire, so each answer gets about a quarter of a page if there are four questions, roughly 80 to 120 handwritten words. (2) Bring two pens of the same ink and correction tape; your phone goes off and into your bag before you sit down. (3) Before you read the first question, lightly divide the back into as many boxes as there are questions, so the first answer cannot eat the space of the last. (4) Write legibly: slow down, print if your cursive gets messy, and use correction tape instead of crossing out. (5) Write your student number only: no name, signature, initials or other marks.

The format you are training for

  • Time: 1 hour 30 minutes for the whole exam (confirmed for this year). With four problems that is about 22 minutes each.
  • Paper: the back of the questionnaire, for all answers (see the rules above).
  • Scoring: each problem is worth 10 points; 70% passes (28 of 40 in 2023).
  • Also from his past instructions: closed book; write legibly (“the examiner is not skilled at deciphering hieroglyphics”); “Verbosity is discouraged. This is an examination, not a dissertation.”
  • Grading standard (2015 instructions): “mastery of the subject matter and … ability to present comprehensive and accurate arguments.” The stories “may have been altered”, so read every fact.

The CLEAR answer (the module Sir Dan shared)

The writing module he circulated (Atty. Tanya Lat, UP College of Law) asks for answers with the three RLs (right law, right logic, right language) and three components (the short answer, its legal basis, and the analysis applying law to facts). The structure that delivers them is CLEAR, the format recommended for bar examinees:

LetterPartHow to write it in Consti 1
CConclusionThe short answer first: “The expulsion is void.” “Section 4 is unconstitutional.” An exam answer is not a suspense novel. You may underline it.
LLegal basisThe provision and the rule: “Under Art. VI, Sec. 16(3) …”. Name a case only if you are sure it is the right one; otherwise write “In one case, the Supreme Court held …”. Cite a section number only if you are certain of it.
EExceptionOnly if an exception actually applies (an exception to mootness; the operative fact doctrine; a permitted delegation). Usually omit it.
AAnalysisApply the rule to the specific facts and the exact words of the law. Non-negotiable: this is where the points are.
RRestatementOne sentence that restates C. It need not repeat it word for word, but it must agree with it.

For a two- or three-issue question, answer all of them in C (“The law is void: it is an undue delegation, and its title is defective.”), then give L and A per issue under short labels. CLEAR is the same idea as the “answer first, then the case” approach his past blocks reported; it adds the exception step and forces C and R to agree.

Six questions before you write (the module)
  1. What is the question being asked? Usually in the last sentence or paragraph.
  2. What issues are relevant to that question?
  3. Which facts are material, and how do the issues relate to them? Some facts are there only to distract.
  4. What legal principles apply: provision, doctrine, case?
  5. How do those principles apply to these facts?
  6. What is your conclusion? Decide it before you write C.
  • The ABCs of legal writing: Accuracy (correct law and cases; a wrong case name costs credibility), Brevity (one correct, focused paragraph can earn full credit), Conciseness (brief but comprehensive; a one-sentence answer is too short).
  • The four pitfalls: the obviously wrong answer (missing the real issue, so everything after it answers the wrong question); the answer that is too long (often hiding that you do not know); the answer that is too short (no application of law to facts); and stream of consciousness (writing before deciding, so the first and last sentences contradict each other).
  • Never answer in one sentence, rely on “the Supreme Court said so”, or be flippant. And do not assume a fact is self-evident: what is obvious to you must still be spelled out for the reader.

The four question shapes he uses

  1. “Determine the constitutionality of Section X of RA ___” with the text quoted (2023 Q3, 2024 Q2 and Q3). Read every clause; the defect is often in one phrase (an oversight committee’s power to “approve”; a mandate on private parties; an IRR’s publication rule).
  2. “Resolve the legal issues raised by this story” or “critique this official’s statement” (2020 quizzes and finals). You must find every issue, procedural and substantive, and say who is right.
  3. “How does the new law address the issues in [case]?” (2024 Q4, Liban and RA 10530). State the issue the case raised, then show how the statute settles it.
  4. “Assume X challenges this; resolve it in his favor”, sometimes with procedural issues ruled out (2023 Q1, Teves). Argue one side as strongly as the law allows; do not hedge.
Gatmaytan: His block intel says to start answers with Yes or No and get straight to the case, which is what CLEAR does. He wants your own view of a case and its historical context; where his book criticizes the Court (Javellana, Pangilinan, severability), say so in one line.

When to write the procedural layer

If the question asks you to resolve a case or a petition, start with a two- or three-line procedural paragraph: proper remedy and forum, actual case and ripeness, standing, and any mootness. Skip it when the question says “do not raise procedural issues”, or asks only whether a provision is constitutional (then one line at most).

Trap: a bill, a draft IRR, or a resolution calling for Charter change is not ripe (Montesclaros; Lozano v. Nograles; In re JDF). If the news is about a proposal, say so first.

The issue checklist

Run this in the margin for every problem. The Issue spotter tab drills it.

  1. Court and procedure: actual case; ripeness or mootness (and its four exceptions); standing (citizen, taxpayer, voter, legislator, association or third party, government, environmental); transcendental importance; earliest opportunity; lis mota; proper remedy (Rule 65, declaratory relief) and forum (SC, RTC, COMELEC, HRET or SET, COA).
  2. Who acted and under what power: Congress (plenary but limited), one House, a committee, the President, an agency, an LGU, a private party.
  3. Separation of powers: encroachment; legislative veto or post-enactment role (ABAKADA, Belgica); Congress interpreting the Constitution or overruling the Court (Endencia); adding qualifications to constitutional offices.
  4. Delegation: completeness and sufficient-standard tests; IRR requisites; publication (Tañada) and filing; subdelegation (KMU).
  5. How the law was made: title and one subject; origination of money bills; riders and inappropriate provisions; savings and augmentation; tax exemptions.
  6. Congress internally: officers, quorum, rules, discipline (60-day cap, two-thirds of all members), privileges, incompatible and forbidden offices, tribunals’ jurisdiction, CA composition.
  7. Inherent powers: police power (lawful subject plus lawful means) versus taking (just compensation); non-impairment; state immunity and money claims.
  8. People: natural-born or naturalized; election of citizenship; RA 9225 steps; residence (domicile); suffrage qualifications versus registration rules.

A model answer that fits the box

2023 Q1 (Teves), in his favor, no procedural issues. About 110 words, in CLEAR.

C The expulsion is void.
L Art. VI, Sec. 16(3) lets the House expel a Member for disorderly behavior with the concurrence of two-thirds of all its Members, and no conviction is required (Osmeña v. Pendatun). But the power must be exercised within the Constitution, including the 60-day cap on suspension.
A Teves’s designation as a terrorist is an executive act, not his conduct as a Member. His absence was already punished by two 60-day suspensions; expelling him for the same absence punishes him twice and evades the cap. A dance video and an asylum bid do not obstruct legislative work, and unproven killings cannot ground the gravest penalty without a real chance to answer them.
R Teves should prevail; the expulsion should be set aside.

Note: E is omitted because no exception applies. This answer argues one side because the question demands it. In a neutral question, you would add that the Court generally defers to the House on what is disorderly (Osmeña).
