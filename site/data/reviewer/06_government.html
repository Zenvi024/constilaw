Government, state immunity, and the inherent powers

Syllabus IV.C: government defined; governmental functions; de facto, de jure and revolutionary government; parens patriae; immunity from suit; money claims; police power. 15 cases. Police power over private businesses was asked in 2023 (cinema free screen time) and 2024 (entrance exam fee waiver).

Government and its functions

  • ‘Government’ is the system and institutions, distinct from the persons administering it (U.S. v. Dorr). Criticism of officials is not an attack on the government.
  • ‘Government of the Philippines’ (Admin. Code, Sec. 2(1)): the corporate governmental entity through which governmental functions are exercised, including its political subdivisions.
  • Constituent functions (compulsory: order, justice, property, contracts) and ministrant functions (optional: welfare, public works) are no longer sharply distinguished. A public office: created by law, delegated sovereign power, continuing, defined by law, for the public benefit (Laurel v. Desierto).

De jure, de facto and revolutionary governments

Three kinds of de facto government (Co Kim Cham): (1) one that usurps the legitimate government by force or the majority’s will; (2) one established by an invading military force (paramount force); (3) one set up by insurgents who secede. Acts of a de facto government not of a political complexion remain valid after restoration (postliminy).

A revolutionary government is bound by no constitution, but the Aquino revolutionary government remained bound by the ICCPR and the Universal Declaration (Republic v. Sandiganbayan).

In a news question: calls for a ‘revolutionary government’ by a sitting president: he was elected under the 1987 Constitution; declaring one repudiates the Constitution that gives him office. Nothing in the Constitution authorizes it.

Parens patriae

The State may protect those who cannot protect themselves (minors, incompetents), but it supplements, not replaces, parents. Restrictions on minors’ fundamental rights are reviewed by strict scrutiny (SPARK v. Quezon City): compelling interest plus least restrictive means.

Immunity from suit

Art. XVI, Sec. 3The State may not be sued without its consent.
Analysis
  1. Is it really a suit against the State? A suit against an officer is against the State if a judgment would require the State to pay or act (Arigo). Not if the officer acted beyond authority, the relief is only to nullify an ultra vires act, or the officer is sued personally for a tort (PASI v. Trinidad-Lichauco).
  2. Has the State consented? Expressly by law, or impliedly when it sues or enters into a commercial (jure gestionis) contract. Incorporated agencies: look at the charter (‘sue and be sued’) (GTZ).
  3. Foreign States and their agents: restrictive immunity covers sovereign acts (jure imperii). Agents acting within official functions, with the host State’s consent, share immunity (Minucher). Warships are immune; the flag State answers internationally (Arigo).
  4. Suability is not liability: even if the State may be sued, public funds cannot be garnished; claims go to COA (Fetalvero). But immunity cannot be used to avoid paying for a completed public work the State has used (Vigilar).

Inherent powers: police power, eminent domain, taxation

Police power is the State’s power to restrain liberty and property to promote the general welfare. It needs no constitutional grant; Congress holds it and delegates it to the President, agencies and LGUs (general welfare clause).

Valid police power (lawful subject and lawful means)
  1. Lawful subject: the interests of the public generally, not of a particular class, require the interference.
  2. Lawful means: the means are reasonably necessary to the purpose and not unduly oppressive on individuals.
For LGUs: the ordinance must also be within the delegated power, consistent with law, and not prohibit (as opposed to regulate) a lawful business (Cruz v. Paras; White Light).
  • Upheld: Mangyan reservation (Rubi, 1919); early warning devices (Agustin v. Edu); Boracay closure (Zabal); expanding a power distributor’s franchise into cooperatives’ areas (Iloilo I Electric Coop., 2024: franchises are never exclusive and yield to the common good).
  • Struck down: short-time motel rates ban (White Light); curfews not narrowly drawn (SPARK).

Regulation or taking?

Police power regulates the use of property without compensation; eminent domain takes property (or its use) for public use and requires just compensation. A regulation becomes a taking when it appropriates property or its beneficial use for the public, rather than restraining a noxious or harmful use.

MeasureClassification
Requiring cemeteries to set aside 6% of their land for paupers (City Government of QC v. Ericta, 1983)Taking without compensation (void)
Requiring newspapers to give COMELEC free print space (Philippine Press Institute v. COMELEC, 1995)Taking; must be paid for
Requiring broadcasters to give COMELEC free airtime (TELEBAP v. COMELEC, 1998)Valid: broadcasters hold franchises over a public resource (airwaves) and the condition is part of the privilege
Senior citizens’ and PWD discounts, deductible from income (Manila Memorial Park v. DSWD, 2013)Valid police power
In a news question (2023: Manila cinemas must give free screen time for city messages; 2024: private HEIs must waive entrance exam fees): (1) Lawful subject? Public information or access to education is a legitimate public interest. (2) Lawful means? Is the burden reasonable and spread fairly, or does it appropriate the private owner’s property (screen time, services) for public use without pay? Cinemas, unlike broadcasters, hold no franchise over a public resource, so PPI and Ericta point to a taking. For schools, add Art. XIV, Sec. 4(1) (reasonable supervision and regulation of educational institutions) and ask whether the waived fee is a modest regulation of a regulated business or an uncompensated appropriation of its services. Answer either way, but apply both tests to the exact words.
Exam trap: calling every burden on a business ‘police power’. Ask whether the State is restraining a use or taking something for public use. Also: non-impairment yields to police power (Iloilo I), but only to a valid exercise of it.
