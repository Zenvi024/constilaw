## How his midterms work

Expect about four essay problems in two hours (30 minutes each), each worth 10 points. You need 70% to pass, and every answer has to fit on one sheet of yellow paper, front and back. Each problem is a real news story or a newly passed law, and he asks you to apply the cases to it. These are the rules on the 2023 midterm. The 2015 finals instructions add: answer from memory with no materials, and "Verbosity is discouraged. This is an examination, not a dissertation."

**What this means for you.** The older exams were multiple choice; since 2020 they have been essays. The same handful of issues keeps coming back:

- what Congress can and cannot do: disciplining members, legislative vetoes and oversight committees, adding qualifications, appropriations and riders
- delegation of power and IRRs
- standing and justiciability
- police power over private parties
- citizenship and party-list

His prompt types fall into four shapes:

1. **"Determine the constitutionality of Section X of RA \_\_\_"**, with the text quoted. Most common in recent midterms.
2. **"Resolve the legal issues raised by this story"** or "critique this official's statement." You must find every issue, procedural and substantive.
3. **"How does the new law address the issues raised in \[case\]?"** This asks you to connect a doctrine to the legislative fix.
4. **"Assume X challenges this; resolve it in his favor"**, sometimes with procedural issues ruled out.

Two instructions to take literally: the stories "may have been altered," so read every fact; and he grades "mastery" and "comprehensive and accurate arguments" within one sheet. Your block's notes say the same: lead with Yes or No, ALAC, concise.

## The 1,176-page masterfile

Don't read it front to back: that is about 25 hours of reading with no practice writing. His exam rewards knowing which doctrine fits which facts, not case details. So the masterfile becomes the source, not the reading.

The masterfile is easy to process. It has 238 digests for 165 cases, a clickable contents list, and consistent FACTS / ISSUE / RULING / RATIO headings. That means an agent can pull each case's holding reliably.

1. **Doctrine sheet (agent builds it; about 25 pages).** For each of the 165 cases: the rule in one or two lines, the test or elements it lays down, and the fact that decided it. Grouped by syllabus heading, built from the RULING and RATIO parts and checked against the 2025 class reviewer and your block's midterm reviewer.
2. **Rule maps (agent builds them; one per syllabus part).** The working reviewer: the provision, the rule, the tests, exceptions, the leading cases in order, how doctrine changed (for example *Araullo* on savings and *Macalintal* 2023), and the usual news patterns that trigger it. This is what you read each day.
3. **Full digests only for anchor cases.** These are about 35 to 40 cases that carry a test (e.g., *Atong Paglaum*, *ABAKADA*, *Belgica*, *Araullo*, *Osmeña v. Pendatun*, *Liban*, *Tolentino v. Sec. of Finance*, *Lung Center*). Also read the full digest for any case you miss twice in drills.

**Skip:** the scanned "DAN GAT 2021 REVIEWER" (it is the same text as the 2025 Reviewer, which can be searched) and Bernas front to back (1,442 pages; look things up there only). Use the Finals Reviewer only for the parts in midterm coverage.

## How to answer in two hours

Plan on 30 minutes per question, and give each answer about half a page of handwriting (roughly 150 to 220 words), since all four must fit on one sheet. Practice to that length from the first day; writing too much is the most likely way to lose time and points.

**Per question (30 minutes):**

1. **Read and mark (4 min).** Underline every fact that could change the answer: who acted, under what power, the exact words of the law, dates, votes. The stories "may have been altered," so check for a planted twist.
2. **Spot the issues (4 min).** Run the checklist below in the margin. Pick the two or three issues that decide the case, and note any procedural ones.
3. **Write (18 min).** Use ALAC: open with the answer ("Section 4 is unconstitutional"). For each issue, state the rule with its provision and one anchor case, apply it to the *specific words* of the law or story, then give a one-line conclusion. Raise the strongest counterargument only if it is serious.
4. **Check (4 min).** Does the answer address the question actually asked ("resolve in his favor," "do not raise procedural issues")?

**Issue checklist** (memorize; the agent will build drills from it):

- **Court and procedure:** actual case, ripeness or mootness, standing (citizen, taxpayer, legislator, association, government), political question, transcendental importance, earliest opportunity, the right remedy and forum
- **Which body acted and under what power:** Congress (plenary, but limited), a committee, one House, the President, an agency, an LGU
- **Separation of powers:** encroachment, legislative veto or post-enactment participation (*ABAKADA*), Congress adding qualifications to constitutional offices
- **Delegation:** completeness and sufficient-standard tests, the IRR and its publication (*Tañada*), subdelegation
- **How the law was made:** title and one subject, origination of revenue and appropriation bills, riders and inappropriate provisions, savings and augmentation
- **Congress internally:** quorum, rules, discipline (suspension of up to 60 days, expulsion by two-thirds), privileges, incompatible and forbidden offices, the electoral tribunals' jurisdiction
- **Inherent powers:** police power (lawful subject and lawful means) versus taking; whether a burden on private businesses is valid regulation or uncompensated taking
- **People:** natural-born versus naturalized status, statutory naturalization, dual citizenship, residence, suffrage limits

## Day by day

There are seven study days, then the exam on Friday, Oct 16. Each block of topics is studied, written on, and drilled on the day you learn it, then revisited in spaced review every day after. Hours are Consti time only; fit them around your other classes. I assumed Consti class still meets Tuesday Oct 13 and Thursday Oct 15 with recitation, so those are lighter days.

**Daily loop:**

- 20 min spaced review of earlier blocks
- Learn the day's block from its rule map, with full digests for anchor cases only
- One or two timed 30-minute answers, handwritten, then 15 minutes grading each against the model answer and rubric
- 10 min adding misses to the error log

## What the agent should build

The new site trains you to apply doctrine and write answers, not to recall provision wording. It reuses the PFR kit's shell (tabs, spaced review, the printable PDF) with different content. The features are listed in build order, so the first ones are ready by Saturday.

1. **Rule maps (Reviewer tab).** One per syllabus part, in the order of section 2. Each gives the provision, the rule, the tests, the leading cases, how doctrine changed, and a box on "how this shows up in a news question." Built from the masterfile, both class reviewers, and Bernas for lookups.
2. **Doctrine sheet and case cards.** One entry per case for all 165. Drills run both ways: case → rule, and fact pattern → which case or test applies. These go into spaced review.
3. **Past-exam bank.** Every question from 2013 to 2024 above, tagged by topic. The essay questions get model answers under 220 words; the old multiple-choice items stay multiple choice with explanations.
4. **Issue spotter.** A news-style fact pattern; you tick every issue it raises from the checklist, then see which ones you missed and the anchor case for each.
5. **Essay trainer.** A 30-minute timer, a word counter set to about 200 words, and a model answer revealed afterward. A rubric you tick yourself: answer stated first, each issue spotted, rule and case named, applied to the facts, conclusion. Prompts come from past exams and new ones the agent writes from current Philippine news (it needs web search for this).&#32;
6. **Mock exams.** Two four-question sets in his format, printable on folio, for Wednesday and Thursday. (User note: no need to implement this yet)
7. **Grading of handwritten answers.** You photograph your answer sheet, upload it to a chat, and the agent grades it against the rubric. This happens in chat; the site only links the rubric. (User note: no need to implement this yet)
8. **One-page printables.** The issue checklist and the anchor-case list, for the morning of the exam.
